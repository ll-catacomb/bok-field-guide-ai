<p className="drop-cap">Every week a new AI tool appears: chat windows, writing assistants, research companions, autonomous agents. They look nothing alike. But they are all built on the same engine — and once you understand it, you can make sense of any tool, current or future, because you know what it is actually doing underneath.</p>

This guide is for teachers, and assumes no coding experience. It starts at the center (the exchange every one of these tools wraps around) and works outward to the specific tools Harvard provides, so that when a colleague mentions "the CLI" or "a custom GPT," you can place it on a map.

## First, what do we mean by "AI"?

<Term id="machine-learning"><em>Machine learning</em></Term>, systems that improve at a task by finding patterns in data, has run spam filters and recommendations for years. What's new is <Term id="generative"><strong>generative</strong></Term> models: systems that produce new text, images, audio, or video.

Even here there is more than one kind of machine. The same companies build <Term id="diffusion"><strong>diffusion models</strong></Term> for images (DALL·E, Imagen); we set those aside. Our subject is the <Term id="llm"><strong>large language model</strong>, or LLM</Term>: the text-in, text-out engine behind Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini. It is what nearly every AI tool reaching your classroom is made of.

We won't cover how an LLM is built or trained. That story is told well elsewhere by Andrej Karpathy, [*Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI).

What you need is a feel for how an LLM actually behaves — what it does well and where it breaks. That is enough to parse what any given AI tool is doing. The short demonstrations below are built to give you that.

## The engine: string in, string out

An LLM is, at its core, a function: a string of text goes in, a string of text comes out.

## It's all tokens

Look again at the bottom row of that demo: the words have turned into numbers. The model never sees letters. Text enters as a sequence of integers called <Term id="token"><strong>tokens</strong></Term>: fragments of text, each mapped to a number. It predicts the next *token*, not the next word.

<Tokenizer />

The splits aren't ours; they reflect what the model saw at scale. This is why models miscount letters and stumble on rare names.

## How your LLM was trained

So how did numbers ever become good text? By prediction, practiced at enormous scale. A model learns exactly one move (*given the text so far, guess what comes next*) and repeats it, billions of times, against a vast library of writing.

The New York Times built a tiny version you can watch learn. Their [*BabyGPT*](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2023/04/26/upshot/gpt-from-scratch.html) is trained on the novels of Jane Austen and does just one thing: predict the next character, over and over, until a sentence forms. At first it produces pure noise. No one programs in spelling, words, or grammar; those emerge, rung by rung, purely from predicting the next character against Austen's prose. Click through its training:

<TrainingProgression />

A small model emits gibberish; trained longer, it finds words, then grammar, then a recognizable voice. Now scale that up. With billions of <Term id="parameters">parameters</Term>, trained on a large fraction of everything ever written, "the likely next token" reads as fluent thought. That is roughly where today's largest models sit. As the *Times* piece shows, nothing here is copied or looked up; the structure of language is *reconstructed*, one prediction at a time.

## The same failure, in words

It isn't only math. Ask a model to close-read a real scene, once cold, once with the text in front of it:

<Grounding />

The model has two hard limits: it can't reliably compute or recall the actual world, and it can only hold so much in view at once. Nearly every tool you'll meet exists to work around those two limits: getting the right material in front of it, and doing something useful with what comes back.

## Everything lands in the context window

Take the second limit: what it can hold. Think of the input as a course pack for a brilliant student who hasn't done the reading: give it the passage, the rubric, an example of the move you want, and it follows your lead. But your prompt, its reply, your next message, and its next all accumulate in one fixed-size <Term id="context-window"><strong>context window</strong></Term>. Spend that space on the few things that actually bear on the task — what the field calls <Term id="high-signal">high-signal</Term> context — and it guesses less. Overfill it and quality drops, because whatever sits in the middle of a long window gets the least attention: the model gets <Term id="lost-in-the-middle">lost in the middle</Term>.

<ContextWindow />

The budget doesn't reset between messages. It fills as the conversation grows. Watch a thread stack up, and see what happens when it runs out of room:

<ContextFill />

When the window fills, most tools <Term id="compaction"><strong>compact</strong></Term> rather than stop: they summarize the older messages and pass the summary forward, so the chat feels seamless. The catch is that the model is now reasoning from a compressed recap with details dropped — the reason a very long thread loses the thread.

## Memory: the first automatic context

If every new chat starts with an empty window, how does a tool ever seem to remember you? By keeping a <Term id="memory"><strong>memory</strong></Term>: a small note it writes and loads into each new thread. It's the simplest form of *automatic context*, context you never pasted, added for you.

<Memory />

## Why the tools exist

Those two limits — what a model can work out, and how much it can hold at once — are what all of these tools are built to work around. Curating what goes in has a name:

<blockquote>
  <p><em>"Good context engineering means finding the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome."</em></p>
  <cite>— Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents"</cite>
</blockquote>

## Handling the output: from copy-paste to automation

That covers what goes in. Getting the answer back out has its own history.

In the earliest way of working, you'd get text back from the chat window, read it, copy it, paste it into a file or a document, and take it somewhere else to see if it worked. If it didn't, you'd go back to the chat, describe what went wrong, and iterate. The model wrote the text; moving it, testing it, and bringing the results back were all your job.

Each of the tools below wraps the engine in a different amount of software. That wrapper has a name — the <Term id="harness"><strong>harness</strong></Term> — and its job is to gather up what goes in and then act on what comes out. A chat window is a thin wrapper: it hands you the text, and the copying, testing, and fixing are yours. A coding agent is a thick one: it takes what the model wrote and does those things itself. Each tool below takes on a little more of that work than the one before. As you read, the useful question is how much you want to see: every step, or only the finished result.

**Chat interfaces** are the exchange at its most literal. You put in a prompt in natural language and read a reply; then you copy what's useful somewhere else to make use of it. *(Claude.ai, ChatGPT, Gemini in the browser.)*

**In-chat live environments** took the first piece of that off your hands: the copying and pasting. Platforms let the output run directly inside the chat: ChatGPT's Code Interpreter, Claude's Artifacts, Gemini's Canvas. Now you could see the output *run* (a chart drawn, a document rendered, a page previewed) without leaving the conversation. The string went in, the string came out, and you could watch it work in the same window.

**Editor and document panels** were the next move. Tools like Cursor, Windsurf, and VS Code with Copilot (or Gemini inside a Google Doc) put the model inside the tool where you already work. Now the model didn't just generate text in a chat bubble; it could read your existing files, understand your project or document, and write changes directly in place. The input got richer (your whole project as context) and the output got more useful (edits applied where you need them, not copy-pasted).

**Command-line tools** took this further. Tools like Claude Code and Codex run in your terminal and can access your entire machine: not just one project, but your file system, your history, your installed programs. The context window now extends to everything on your local device, and the output can be written anywhere.

**All-in-one platforms** like Lovable, Replit, and Bolt approach it from the other direction. Instead of giving programmers more control, they take the technical work away from people who aren't programmers. These tools handle not just the writing but the setup and the hosting. You describe what you want and the platform carries it all the way to a running application, without your ever seeing the machinery.

<Term id="agent"><strong>Autonomous agents</strong></Term> are the final step, so far. Agent modes in Cursor and Claude Code (and standalone agents) don't wait for your prompts. You give them your files, your tools, your passwords, and a memory that carries over, and they drive. You don't control the string in or the string out; you rarely even see the intermediate steps. You just see the final product. This is powerful, and it is genuinely dangerous: an agent can rack up costs, introduce errors, and make irreversible changes without asking.

The *engine* never changed. A string goes into a model and a string comes out, in every one of these. What changes is how much software sits between you and that exchange — and whether, for the task in front of you, it is helping or hiding the work.

## Tool calls: giving the model hands and eyes

The mechanism that lets a harness do any of this is the <Term id="tool-call"><strong>tool call</strong></Term>. A tool is any function the model is allowed to invoke, and in every case the model supplies the *judgment* — it recognizes it needs a tool and writes the call — while the tool supplies the *precision*.

You've already seen one. The Python run in the multiplication demo was a tool call: the model couldn't compute the product, so it wrote code (the code is its *output*) and a code executor ran that code and handed the real answer back. The calculation happened in Python, not in the model.

Tools come in two flavors, along the same input/output axis we've been using: tools that give the model **hands** (to *do* more) and tools that give it **eyes** (to *see* more):

<ToolCategories />

Most tools are ordinary pieces of software, and they give the model abilities it genuinely doesn't have on its own. The companies ship the "right" tools and the documentation for them. And, as we'll see, you can add your own.

### Unpacking a tool call: RAG on a PDF

Retrieval, the most important of the eyes-side tools, is both powerful and failure-prone. It exists because the context window is finite. A small document you can simply paste in whole, and the model sees every word. But scale up, and the window fills. A whole book, let alone a library, won't come close to fitting:

<RetrievalTradeoff />

That overflow is what <Term id="rag"><strong>RAG</strong></Term> (retrieval-augmented generation) is built for. Instead of loading everything, it loads only the passages most relevant to your question:

<RagPipeline />

#### What makes a chunk "relevant"?

That word "relevant" has a precise, mechanical meaning. Every <Term id="chunk">chunk</Term> (and your question) is turned into a <Term id="embedding"><strong>vector</strong></Term>: a list of numbers that fixes it at a point in space, so that words and passages used in similar ways land near each other. *King* and *queen* sit almost on top of one another; *graphene* is off in a distant region entirely. "Relevant" just means *near*. (The technique that first made this work at scale was **word2vec**; you can explore real, high-dimensional embeddings in [the TensorFlow Embedding Projector](https://projector.tensorflow.org/).)

But that arrangement is a *particular* way of organizing language, not always the *right* one. Where the exact word is the argument (law, medicine, close textual scholarship), near-synonyms like *murder* and *manslaughter* sit side by side in vector space yet are anything but interchangeable. This is why RAG can fail careful academic work: it retrieves what is *similar*, not necessarily what is *right*.

This whole pipeline (window, chunks, vectors, retrieval) is exactly what **NotebookLM, Claude Projects, and custom GPTs** are doing under the hood.

### Reaching out: APIs, keys, and MCP

The eyes and hands so far have mostly stayed inside the chat. But the most powerful tools reach *out*, to a live service — a Google Doc, a course database, your calendar. The mechanism is an <Term id="api"><strong>API</strong></Term> (application programming interface): a service publishes a set of requests it will answer, and the harness sends one, proving it's allowed with a <Term id="api-key"><strong>secret key</strong></Term>, a password for machines. The service checks the key and sends the data back.

<ApiHandshake />

That works beautifully for one service. The trouble is that every service is different: a different API, a different set of methods, its own documentation, its own key, and all of those change over time. Wiring a harness to a dozen services means a dozen custom connections that someone has to build and keep repairing.

<ApiTools />

<Term id="mcp"><strong>MCP</strong></Term> (the **Model Context Protocol**) is the fix, and it works the way a standard plug shape does. Everyone agrees on one shape. Each service then gets a small adapter that speaks that shape on the outside and the service's own language on the inside, and holds that service's password. The AI tool only ever has to learn the one shape. Connecting something new — your library catalog, a lab instrument, a university system — becomes a matter of plugging in another adapter rather than rebuilding the tool. It is what turns "the model can use this" into something you, and not only the company that sold you the tool, can add to.
