<p className="drop-cap">Every week a new AI tool appears: chat windows, writing assistants, research companions, autonomous agents. They look nothing alike. But they are all built on the same engine — and once you understand it, you can make sense of any tool, current or future, because you know what it is actually doing underneath.</p>

This guide is for teachers, and assumes no code. It starts at the center (the exchange every one of these tools wraps around) and works outward to the specific tools Harvard provides, so that when a colleague mentions "the CLI" or "a custom GPT," you can place it on a map.

## First, what do we mean by "AI"?

<Term id="machine-learning"><em>Machine learning</em></Term>, systems that improve at a task by finding patterns in data, has run spam filters and recommendations for years. What's new is <Term id="generative"><strong>generative</strong></Term> models: systems that produce new text, images, audio, or video.

Even here there is more than one kind of machine. The same companies build <Term id="diffusion"><strong>diffusion models</strong></Term> for images (DALL·E, Imagen); we set those aside. Our subject is the <Term id="llm"><strong>large language model</strong>, or LLM</Term>: the text-in, text-out engine behind Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini. It is what nearly every AI tool reaching your classroom is made of.

We won't cover how an LLM is built or trained. That story is told well elsewhere by Andrej Karpathy, [*Deep Dive into LLMs like ChatGPT*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7xTGNNLPyMI).

What you need is a feel for the materiality of LLMs (the "under the hood" understanding of processes, the strengths and constraints, etc.). This should be enough to get your bearings and to parse what a given AI tool is actually doing. A few short demonstrations follow to help give you exactly those intuitions.

## The engine: string in, string out

An LLM is, at its core, a function: a string of text goes in, a string of text comes out.

Try the exchange, then flip it to see what the model holds:

<StringInStringOut />

Every tool in this guide packages operations around this one exchange. Text in, text out; everything else is scaffolding.

## It's all tokens

Flip that demo to "what the model sees" and the words become numbers, literally. The model never sees letters. Text enters as a sequence of integers called <Term id="token"><strong>tokens</strong></Term>: fragments of text, each mapped to a number. It predicts the next *token*, not the next word.

<Tokenizer />

The splits aren't ours; they reflect what the model saw at scale. This is why models miscount letters and stumble on rare names.

## How your LLM was trained

So how did numbers ever become good text? By prediction, practiced at enormous scale. A model learns exactly one move (*given the text so far, guess what comes next*) and repeats it, billions of times, against a vast library of writing.

The New York Times built a tiny version you can watch learn. Their [*BabyGPT*](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2023/04/26/upshot/gpt-from-scratch.html) is trained on the novels of Jane Austen and does just one thing: predict the next character, over and over, until a sentence forms. At first it produces pure noise. No one programs in spelling, words, or grammar; those emerge, rung by rung, purely from predicting the next character against Austen's prose. Click through its training:

<TrainingProgression />

A small model emits gibberish; trained longer, it finds words, then grammar, then a recognizable voice. Now scale that up. With billions of <Term id="parameters">parameters</Term>, trained on a large fraction of everything ever written, "the likely next token" reads as fluent thought. That is roughly where today's frontier models sit. As the *Times* piece shows, nothing here is copied or looked up; the structure of language is *reconstructed*, one prediction at a time.

## The same failure, in words

It isn't only math. Ask a model to close-read a real scene, once cold, once with the text in front of it:

<Grounding />

The model has two hard limits: it can't reliably compute or recall the actual world, and it can only hold so much in view at once. Nearly every tool you'll meet exists to manage those two limits — feeding it the right inputs, and carrying its outputs.

## Everything lands in the context window

Take the second limit: what it can hold. Think of the input as a course pack for a brilliant student who hasn't done the reading: give it the passage, the rubric, an example of the move you want, and it follows your lead. But your prompt, its reply, your next message, and its next all accumulate in one fixed-size <Term id="context-window"><strong>context window</strong></Term>. Spend that budget on high-signal context and it guesses less; overspend and quality drops as the model gets lost in the middle.

<ContextWindow />

The budget doesn't reset between messages. It fills as the conversation grows. Watch a thread stack up, and see what happens when it runs out of room:

<ContextFill />

When the window fills, most tools <Term id="compaction"><strong>compact</strong></Term> rather than stop: they summarize the older turns and pass the summary forward, so the chat feels seamless. The catch is that the model is now reasoning from a compressed, lossy recap — the reason a very long thread loses the thread.

## Memory: the first automatic context

If every new chat opens a blank window, how does a tool ever seem to remember you? By keeping a <Term id="memory"><strong>memory</strong></Term>: a small note it writes and loads into each new thread. It's the simplest form of *automatic context*, context you never pasted, added for you.

<Memory />

## Why the tools exist

The model's limits and its finite window are what the whole ecosystem of tools is built to manage, and the space you work in as an applied user. Curating that input well has a name:

<blockquote>
  <p><em>"Good context engineering means finding the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome."</em></p>
  <cite>— Anthropic, "Building Effective Agents"</cite>
</blockquote>

## Handling the output: from copy-paste to automation

Context solves the input side. But what about the output?

In the earliest way of working, you'd get text back from the chat window, read it, copy it, paste it into a file or a document, and take it somewhere else to see if it worked. If it didn't, you'd go back to the chat, describe what went wrong, and iterate. The string came out of the model, and then *you* had to carry it the rest of the way.

Each tier is a different <Term id="harness"><strong>harness</strong></Term>: the software wrapping the engine that manages your context and does the carrying for you, thin in a chat box and thick in a coding agent. The evolution of these tools is largely the story of automating that carry. As you read the tiers below, ask one question: *do I want to see what's happening, or have it handled for me?* The right answer changes with the stakes.

**Chat interfaces** are the exchange at its most literal. You put in a prompt in natural language and read a reply; then you copy what's useful somewhere else to make use of it. String-in, string-out at its most direct. *(Claude.ai, ChatGPT, Gemini in the browser.)*

**In-chat live environments** were the first step toward automating the carry. Platforms added inline execution directly inside the chat: ChatGPT's Code Interpreter, Claude's Artifacts, Gemini's Canvas. Now you could see the output *run* (a chart drawn, a document rendered, a page previewed) without leaving the conversation. The string went in, the string came out, and you could watch it work in the same window.

**Editor and document panels** were the next move. Tools like Cursor, Windsurf, and VS Code with Copilot (or Gemini inside a Google Doc) put the model inside the tool where you already work. Now the model didn't just generate text in a chat bubble; it could read your existing files, understand your project or document, and write changes directly in place. The input got richer (your whole project as context) and the output got more useful (edits applied where you need them, not copy-pasted).

**Command-line tools** took this further. Tools like Claude Code and Codex run in your terminal and can access your entire machine: not just one project, but your file system, your history, your installed programs. The context window now extends to everything on your local device, and the output can be written anywhere.

**All-in-one platforms** like Lovable, Replit, and Bolt approach it from the other direction. Instead of giving developers more control, they give non-developers less friction. These tools handle not just the generation but the deployment, the setup, the provisioning. You describe what you want and the platform handles the entire pipeline: input to output to a running application. You don't need to know what a build system is.

<Term id="agent"><strong>Autonomous agents</strong></Term> are the final step, so far. Agent modes in Cursor and Claude Code (and standalone agents) don't wait for your prompts. You give them access to your files, your tools, and your keys (the proverbial keys to the car) and a persistent memory, and they drive. You don't control the string in or the string out; you rarely even see the intermediate steps. You just see the final product. This is powerful, and it is genuinely dangerous: an agent can rack up costs, introduce errors, and make irreversible changes without asking.

Notice that the *engine* never changed. A string goes into a model and a string comes out, at every tier. What changes is how thick the harness is: how many layers sit between you and that exchange, and whether, for the task at hand, they help or hide.

## Tool calls: giving the model hands and eyes

Now that we understand how harnesses automatically handle your inputs and outputs, we can name the mechanism they use to do it: the <Term id="tool-call"><strong>tool call</strong></Term>. A tool is any function the model is allowed to invoke, and in every case the model supplies the *judgment* — it recognizes it needs a tool and writes the call — while the tool supplies the *precision*.

You've already seen one. The Python run in the multiplication demo was a tool call: the model couldn't compute the product, so it wrote code (the code is its *output*) and a code executor ran that code and handed the real answer back. The calculation happened in Python, not in the model.

Tools come in two flavors, along the same input/output axis we've been using: tools that give the model **hands** (to *do* more) and tools that give it **eyes** (to *see* more):

<ToolCategories />

Most tools are code or API based, and they give the model abilities it genuinely doesn't have on its own. The companies ship the "right" tools and the documentation for them. And, as we'll see, you can add your own.

### Unpacking a tool call: RAG on a PDF

The eyes-side tools are worth slowing down on, because the most important one — retrieval — is both powerful and failure-prone. Start with the reason it exists: the context window is finite. A small document you can simply paste in whole, and the model sees every word. But scale up, and the window fills. A whole book, let alone a library, won't come close to fitting:

<RetrievalTradeoff />

That overflow is what <Term id="rag"><strong>RAG</strong></Term> (retrieval-augmented generation) is built for. Instead of loading everything, it loads only the passages most relevant to your question:

<RagPipeline />

#### What makes a chunk "relevant"?

Everything hinges on that word "relevant," and it has a precise, mechanical meaning. Every <Term id="chunk">chunk</Term> (and your question) is turned into a <Term id="embedding"><strong>vector</strong></Term>: a list of numbers that fixes it at a point in space, so that words and passages used in similar ways land near each other. *King* and *queen* sit almost on top of one another; *graphene* is off in a distant region entirely. "Relevant" just means *near*. (The technique that first made this work at scale was **word2vec**; you can explore real, high-dimensional embeddings in [the TensorFlow Embedding Projector](https://projector.tensorflow.org/).)

But that arrangement is a *particular* way of organizing language, not always the *right* one. Where the exact word is the argument (law, medicine, close textual scholarship), near-synonyms like *murder* and *manslaughter* sit side by side in vector space yet are anything but interchangeable. This is the deep reason RAG can fail careful academic work: it retrieves what is *similar*, not necessarily what is *right*.

This whole pipeline (window, chunks, vectors, retrieval) is exactly what **NotebookLM, Claude Projects, and custom GPTs** are doing under the hood.

### Reaching out: APIs, keys, and MCP

The eyes and hands so far have mostly stayed inside the chat. But the most powerful tools reach *out*, to a live service — a Google Doc, a course database, your calendar. The mechanism is an <Term id="api"><strong>API</strong></Term> (application programming interface): a service publishes a set of requests it will answer, and the harness sends one, proving it's allowed with a <Term id="api-key"><strong>secret key</strong></Term>, a password for machines. The service checks the key and sends the data back.

<ApiHandshake />

That works beautifully for one service. The trouble is that every service is different: a different API, a different set of methods, its own documentation, its own key, and all of those change over time. Wiring a harness to a dozen services means a dozen bespoke, brittle integrations that someone has to build and keep repairing.

<ApiTools />

This is the problem <Term id="mcp"><strong>MCP</strong></Term> (the **Model Context Protocol**) was built to solve. It's an open standard, best thought of as a universal port (a USB-C for tools). Instead of teaching the harness every service's private API, you run a small **MCP server** for each service; the server holds that service's key and translates the one standard protocol into the service's own calls. The harness only ever needs to speak MCP. Adding a new capability (your library catalog, a lab instrument, a university system) becomes "plug in another server," not "rewrite the app." It's how "the model can use this" becomes something you, not just the vendor, can extend.
