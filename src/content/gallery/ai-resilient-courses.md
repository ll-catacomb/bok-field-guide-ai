Generative AI has changed the conditions under which traditional take-home assignments are completed, and faculty across the FAS are looking for principled ways to respond without giving up the formats they value. The Bok Center is recommending four strategies, each adaptable to a wide range of disciplines and course sizes: bringing key assessments into the classroom (written or oral); pairing take-home work with brief, impromptu in-person comprehension checks; scaffolding large assignments with staged, in-person checkpoints; and anchoring take-home work in inputs or outputs that AI cannot easily reproduce — primary materials and student-collected evidence on the input side, modality-specific artifacts like video, podcast, or performance on the output side. None of the four is meant to prevent AI use; each is meant to ensure that what students submit reflects understanding they can stand behind, and to recenter courses on the disciplinary work the assignments were designed to develop. The Center supports faculty adoption in three complementary ways: one-on-one consultations tailored to a specific course, collaborations to co-design assignments and run department- or course-level workshops, and self-service web resources for faculty and TFs who cannot attend live sessions. The work is anchored institutionally in three *Pressing Pedagogical Challenge* working groups — on oral assessment and homework verification, on reading in the context of AI, and on AI-resilient writing and research assignments — whose outputs feed back into the consultations, workshops, and online resources we offer the rest of the FAS.

The arrival of widely available generative AI has not changed what FAS faculty are trying to teach, but it has changed the conditions under which many traditional assignments are completed. Problem sets, response papers, translations, long-form essays, and short writing exercises remain pedagogically valuable formats; what is newly uncertain is whether the work submitted in those formats reflects the student's own understanding. The Bok Center's AI-resilience work is built around a simple principle: rather than abandon these formats or police tool use, redesign the conditions of assessment so that submitted work is anchored to demonstrated understanding the student can stand behind.

Across pilots, working groups, and consultations, the Center is recommending four adaptable strategies.

**1. In-class assessment of skills previously assessed only through take-home work.** Where a particular skill is central to a course's learning goals, faculty can move its assessment into the classroom — in writing, on paper, or through structured oral exchanges. The clearest current example is **GENED 1196** (Sarah Craycraft), where live oral assessments are the primary assessment format. The course is able to use oral assessment at scale because of software the Bok Center has prototyped that uses AI to generate fresh follow-up questions tied to each student's submitted work, making the format both more rigorous and more practical for large enrollments.

**2. Impromptu understanding checks of out-of-class work.** Take-home work can be paired with brief, low-stakes, in-person checks — written on paper or conducted as a short oral exchange — that ask students to explain, extend, or reflect metacognitively on what they submitted. **COMPSCI 20** (Rebecca Nesson) is the leading example: after each problem set, students complete a short paper-based follow-up that asks them to explain a step in their proof, extend a result, or reflect on which problem was hardest. **FRENCH 10/11** (Nicole Mills) and **HIST-LIT 93AA** (Lauren Kaminsky) are adapting the same pattern to language and writing instruction, often with help from the same AI-supported question prototype. **SCRB 111: Regenerative Biology** (Jessica Whited) extends the model to a different submission format altogether — student-produced video — pairing each in-class screening with live questions generated by a Bok Center AI pipeline that draws claim anchors from the video transcript and produces lay-audience-calibrated prompts for the student to answer in front of peers. Whited reports that all six Fall 2025 groups found the format more rewarding than a traditional final, and that — as one student put it — it is "difficult to 100% chat a video you have to defend live." When the prompts ask for reflection, extension, or transfer rather than rote recall, the check is itself a learning activity — and the prospect of such a prompt incentivizes students to actually do the thinking the assignment was designed to elicit.

**3. Scaffolding large assignments with staged, in-person checkpoints.** For long-form writing and research, the Bok Center is helping faculty break monolithic assignments into stages — proposal, outline, source review, draft, revision — each of which includes an in-person touch point with a TF or instructor. **HIST-LIT 90FK** (Sohm) is the current course pilot: scaffolded checkpoints are integrated into the long-form paper sequence, designed to preserve the intellectual aims of extended writing while making AI shortcuts pedagogically unproductive. The *AI-Resilient Writing and Research Assignments* working group (Ann Blair, Jane Rosenzweig) is producing shared guidance for this strategy that any writing-intensive course can adopt.

**4. Assignments anchored in material AI can't easily reproduce.** A take-home assignment becomes meaningfully harder to outsource when it is anchored to particular evidence — *inputs* the student themselves engaged with — or required to produce a particular kind of artifact — *outputs* that bear the student's own voice, body, or hand. **GENED 1105: Can We Know Our Past?** (Rowan Flad, Jason Ur) illustrates the input side: students apply archaeological methods to a specific physical object they have photogrammetrically scanned themselves, using a Bok Center 3D-scanning workflow built for the course, and build a scaffolded StoryMap capstone around that object's history. The evidentiary base of every paper is something the student personally engaged with. On the output side, the writing program's **EXPOS 20** video-essay program — running across many sections (Pietras, Strauss, Musselman, Raymer, Gauvreau, and others) with in-studio Bok Center workshops on editing, voice, and visual argument — has students remediate their own research papers and primary-source readings into 6-to-9 minute video essays. Because the video must be produced by the student in a recognizable visual and narrative voice, and because it grows directly out of a research paper the student has already written, the format resists generic AI output on its face. The principle generalizes well beyond these two: podcasts in **EASTD 111** and **HISTSCI 1110**, films in **FRENCH 40**, science-communication video essays in **HEB 162** and **GENED 1145**, microphone and performance work in **EXPOS 40**, and creative-medium capstones with required critical artist's statements all instantiate the same underlying logic — the more clearly an assignment requires the student's own evidence, voice, and hand, the harder it is to bypass with a prompt.

These strategies are not mutually exclusive, and most successful redesigns combine two or more — a scaffolded paper sequence with impromptu checks at each stage, for example, or an in-class oral exam built around a take-home dataset the student has personally collected.

The Bok Center supports faculty in adopting these strategies in three complementary ways. **Individual consultations** are the most common entry point: a faculty member meets with Center staff to think through a specific assignment, course, or syllabus, and leaves with concrete revisions tailored to their pedagogy. **Collaborations** go deeper — co-designing assignments with the instructor, building any supporting infrastructure (sample prompts, grading rubrics, prototype software where useful), running workshops in the department or for the course's TFs, and following the work through into the term. And for faculty and TFs who cannot attend live sessions, a growing library of **self-service web resources** captures the patterns, templates, and worked examples coming out of the pilots and working groups, so that the lessons of one course can travel to many.

This work is anchored institutionally in three *Pressing Pedagogical Challenge* working groups, each focused on a defined instructional problem: **Oral Assessment and Homework Verification** (Kaminsky, Craycraft, and others), **Assigning and Assessing Reading in the Context of AI** (Elmer, Carranza, with Rennix from the ARC), and **AI-Resilient Writing and Research Assignments** (Blair, Rosenzweig). The groups produce guidance that flows directly into the consultations and resources we offer the rest of the FAS — turning the lessons of individual pilots into shared FAS practice.
