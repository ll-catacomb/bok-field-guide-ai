ASTRO 17 is one of the first Harvard FAS science courses to integrate generative AI as a stated learning goal alongside its disciplinary content: the syllabus lists "develop proficiency with generative AI tools, including understanding their limitations and shortcomings" as a fourth learning goal, on equal footing with mastering extragalactic astronomy, applying mechanics to galaxies and cosmology, and fitting models to data. To make that goal concrete, the Learning Lab worked with Professor Stubbs to integrate AI across several aspects of the course on the student side, including unguided AI exploration, a RAG-grounded Slack channel built on the OpenAI Assistants API and the textbook *An Introduction to Galaxies and Cosmology*, AI-supported presentation preparation, learning prompts embedded in Colab notebooks, hybrid assignments split into AI-allowed and AI-discouraged sections, AI-assisted code generation and debugging in Colab, and a final-exam review Gem that drew only from uploaded course materials. The pedagogical counterweight to all of this was a deliberately old-fashioned assessment regime, with proctored midterm and final exams that prohibited electronics and were weighted to roughly half the course grade, so that students would invest in foundational mastery rather than route around it. On the instructor side, AI was used to generate sophisticated Colab worksheets and interactive plots, draft and refine reading-quiz and exam questions, "take" draft exams to surface ambiguous wording, and assemble the course's GitHub repository in under an hour using Claude Code. Mid-course assessment combined a Likert survey (N=5 of 14), an in-class focus group, a differential comparison on midterm performance between AI- and non-AI-linked questions, and end-of-term course evaluations; students rated code generation and debugging as the highest-value use of AI (M=4.40) and unguided self-study with AI as the lowest (M=2.60), and overall course satisfaction was a sigma above the prior non-AI year. Alongside the curricular work, the Learning Lab worked to secure IRB approval to formally study student responses to the AI integrations, which served as a pilot for what it takes to set up faculty/Learning-Lab co-designed classroom research on novel AI interventions. The collaboration's most durable output is a lessons-learned paper, currently being prepared for publication, that names what worked, what did not, and what surprised the instructional team, most pointedly the underuse of the Slack channel and the discovery that second-year undergraduates do not share faculty intuitions about AI's centrality to modern science unless those intuitions are explicitly motivated in week one.

Christopher Stubbs's Fall 2025 offering of ASTRO 17, "Extragalactic Astronomy and Cosmology," is the second half of Harvard's two-semester introductory series for prospective astrophysics majors, and was the first time the course had been taught with AI threaded through it as a matter of policy rather than as an exception. The Learning Lab worked alongside the instructional team across the entire term, on both the design of the AI-augmented learning surfaces and the assessment apparatus that would let the instructional team and the broader Bok Center understand what had actually worked. The collaboration had two key deliverables: a working course that 14 undergraduate students completed, and a lessons-learned paper currently being prepared for publication (Stubbs, Huang, Koh, Woods, & Plazas Malagón) that documents the integration with enough specificity that other faculty can borrow the pieces.

The starting point was the syllabus. ASTRO 17's stated learning goals were rewritten to put AI proficiency on equal footing with the disciplinary content: the course would teach extragalactic astronomy *and* the practice of using AI tools well, including their limitations. The accompanying course AI policy framed AI as a learning enhancer that could "instill a false sense of competence if used to short-circuit the intellectual engagement, and yes intellectual struggle, that leads to deeper understanding." The policy committed the course to substantial closed-book, no-electronics components on the midterm and final exams, and to a clear distinction between "AI-encouraged" and "AI-discouraged" sections of weekly assignments. Students were told explicitly that the AI-discouraged problems were the ones whose shapes they would see on the exam.

What sat on top of that policy was a portfolio of student-facing AI integrations spanning several aspects of the course, which the paper documents in roughly the order of student-perceived value: a final-exam review Gem (deployed late, with limited evaluation data); unguided AI-assisted exploration (rated lowest by students); an AI-linked Slack channel (built on the OpenAI Assistants API with the textbook *An Introduction to Galaxies and Cosmology* uploaded as a RAG knowledge base, rated low by students despite being one of the most technically ambitious pieces of the course); guided AI-assisted preparation for short chalkboard presentations on narrow topics like photometric redshifts (rated higher, in keeping with the broader pattern that AI was more useful when the scope of inquiry was tighter); learning prompts embedded directly in Colab notebooks alongside code cells (rated favorably); hybrid assignments split into AI-allowed and AI-discouraged sections (rated near the top); and Colab-embedded AI for code generation, debugging, and explanation (rated highest, M=4.40 on a 5-point scale).

The instructor-side use of AI ran in parallel and was, in some ways, the larger productivity story. The instructional team used NotebookLM with the textbook PDF as persistent context to generate first-draft reading-quiz questions for each module, edited the drafts, and ingested them into Canvas. They drafted bank multiple-choice questions for the final exam and a corresponding practice exam in the same way. They submitted draft exams to a fresh AI chat session and asked it to "take" the exam, which repeatedly surfaced ambiguously worded questions whose intent the AI had misread, exactly the failure mode the instructional team needed to catch before the exam reached students. They generated dynamic interactive Colab cells (slider-driven plots over real Rubin Observatory photometry of galaxies around Abell 360, used to teach photometric redshift estimation) at a level of sophistication that would otherwise have been inaccessible to a small introductory course. And at the end of the term, they used Claude Code to clean the full set of accumulated Colab notebooks, standardize their data-access patterns, test them, scaffold a GitHub repository, and generate a LaTeX instructor's guide. That end-to-end packaging operation took about an hour, and, the paper notes, almost certainly would not have happened at all without that productivity jump.

Alongside the in-course teaching work, the Learning Lab pursued IRB approval at the start of the term to formally study student responses to the AI integrations. Approval came through too late in the semester to run the full study the team had designed, and the assessment apparatus had to be scaled back to a lighter-weight version that did not require the protocol. The IRB workstream nonetheless served a second purpose for the Lab: it was a working pilot of what it takes to co-design classroom research with a faculty partner on a novel AI intervention, covering consent flows, instrument design, the audit trail the IRB expects, and the timeline pressures classroom research has to absorb. The clearest forward-looking lesson is that IRB approval for term-long AI research has to be secured before the semester begins, and the Learning Lab is now substantially better positioned to help the next faculty partner meet that bar.

The assessment apparatus combined a Likert sentiment survey (returned by 5 of 14 students), a 20-minute in-class focus group facilitated by Bok researchers with the instructors out of the room, a differential comparison of midterm performance between AI-linked and non-AI-linked questions (no statistically significant gap), the final exam itself, and end-of-term course evaluations (returned by 11 of 14 students, with overall satisfaction a sigma above the prior non-AI year). Two findings from that work matter most for other faculty considering similar integrations. The first is that students rated AI use highest when it was concrete, structured, and instructor-sanctioned (code generation, AI-allowed homework, embedded notebook prompts) and rated it lowest when it was open-ended and self-directed (unguided study, the public Slack channel). The second is that the Slack channel, despite being among the most technically ambitious pieces of the course, was substantially underused, apparently because students did not want to ask clarification questions in a publicly visible space, a finding consistent with reports from a parallel HBS deployment that allowed private DMs and saw heavy uptake. The instructional team also reported a more interpretive surprise: second-year undergraduates did not share their professor's intuition that AI is becoming an integral part of modern science. Student sympathy for the course's AI-forward design rose noticeably after a mid-semester lecture in which the instructor laid out *why* AI was integrated the way it was, and the instructional team's clearest piece of forward advice is that this lecture should have come in week one.

The strategic frame this work sits inside is the Bok Center's broader stance that AI integration into a course is most defensible when it is paired with a clear-eyed account of what AI is *not* doing. In ASTRO 17's case, that meant the no-electronics midterm and final, the AI-discouraged half of every assignment, and the explicit syllabus statement that AI use must be cited and that the student bears final responsibility for output. ASTRO 17 is now one of the most thoroughly documented examples Harvard has of what whole-course AI integration looks like in a STEM context, and the lessons-learned paper, once published, is structured so that any instructor designing a similar course can read off the menu (what to try, what surprised us, what we would do differently) without having to repeat the experiments themselves.
