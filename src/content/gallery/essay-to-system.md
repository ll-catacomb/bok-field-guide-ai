The Bok Center's design pattern, repeated across courses as different as comparative literature, law, language, music, and the sciences, replaces the final paper with a *system* the student designs and defends: a curated *corpus* (the set of primary materials the student has assembled and indexed for the system to operate on), a chain of operations or AI *agents* (single-purpose programs the student configures, each performing one disciplinary move) that the student names and orders, an output the system produces, and a written or oral defense of every step. Two parts of that pattern are AI-resistant by design. The **input** requires embodied work that does not exist in any model's training data or in a web search — a photograph the student took at the Harvard Art Museums, a card-by-card externalization of their own translation practice, a recorded oral history they conducted themselves. The **output** is defended in person, in registers art and design schools have always used: the *crit* (a public discussion in which the student stands beside their work while peers and instructors interrogate the choices made), the *oral defense* (a one-on-one or small-panel conversation in which the student justifies the work), the *under-the-hood quiz* (a short follow-up that asks the student to walk through a specific decision in their own submission), and the *artist's statement* (a short written piece in which the student explains what they made, why, and what they learned). None of these is new; what is new is bringing them into disciplines where the scaffolded essay has carried the assessment burden alone. The pattern is the connective tissue between these two anchors; the artifact the student finally turns in is at once a learning vehicle, an assessment instrument, and structured evidence the course infrastructure can act on, in a way the AI era cannot quietly produce.

The required first-year writing course at Harvard is roughly 150 years old, and the intellectual technology at its center — the scaffolded academic essay — carried three professional functions on a single artifact for most of that span: it taught students how to think within a discipline, it assessed whether the student had learned, and it matched the form of academic communication the professor was already producing in their own research. Student learning, faculty assessment, and disciplinary publication all rode on the same object.

The condition that held that alignment together is no longer entirely intact. When the artifact a student turns in can be produced by a tool the student has access to and the instructor cannot reliably detect, the inferential link between the artifact and the student's learning weakens. Teaching and disciplinary publication still ride on the essay more or less as they always did; the assessment function is the one that has come loose. Faculty across the curriculum are now in the position of needing to decompose, more explicitly than they have had to in a century and a half, what the essay was tacitly carrying — and to rebuild those functions across new combinations of instruments.

The Learning Lab's working answer to *what comes after the paper* is a design pattern, repeated across courses as different as comparative literature, law, language, music, history, and the sciences. The pattern replaces the final paper with a *system* the student designs and defends. The system has four parts: a *corpus* the student curates (the primary materials the student has assembled and indexed for the system to operate on), a chain of operations or *agents* the student names and orders (single-purpose AI programs, each configured to perform one disciplinary move), an output the system produces, and a written or oral defense of the whole sequence. Each part is assessable in its own right. What counts as a good corpus is different from what counts as a good decomposition, which is different from what counts as a good output, which is different from what counts as a good defense. The structural choice that makes the pattern carry assessment weight is to grade the *defense* numerically against a rubric while the creative artifact itself is graded complete/incomplete. The student is graded on the articulation of what they did, why they did it, and what the disciplinary lesson is — exactly the part of the work AI cannot quietly produce.

Two parts of the pattern are AI-resistant by design, and the discipline of designing AI-resilient assignments comes down, in practice, to making both of them load-bearing.

The first is the **input**. AI cannot generate strings that do not exist in its training data, and it cannot produce a context that did not happen. Madeleine Woods, the Lab's Assistant Director for AI Initiatives, articulated this in the Lab's recent Google visit: *there are some strings that just don't exist within the training data, don't even exist in a web search, there's no context that you can create that could replace an embodied experience in the world.* The corollary for assignment design is that the corpus the student assembles for their system has to come, in part, from physical or experiential work the student in fact did. In COMPLIT 126x's Imaginary Museum project — a unit in which students photograph an object at the Harvard Art Museums and run an iterative text-image-text chain on it — students take their own photograph of the object and record their own voice speaking about it in the gallery, and use those embodied artifacts as the basis of the *prompt chain* (a sequence of AI calls in which each call's output becomes the next call's input, with the student naming each step). *If they did not physically go in person to select their object, they're missing all of these different little inputs.* In ANE 140, students work with Hebrew primary sources whose authority sits in physical archives, with AI as a comparative annotator that surfaces linguistic gaps and "non-human" literal readings the student then has to interpret against the source. In TS 280, each graduate-student translator writes their own moves of translation onto physical index cards — one move per card — and photographs them; the cards are then converted into cells in a Python notebook, so the student can see their own translator's mental model running as a computational pipeline. In AFRAMER 223 (an oral-history methods course), the input is a recorded oral history the student conducted themselves. The pattern is the same in each case: the assignment requires the student to bring in something AI cannot have already generated, and the student's act of going to get it is itself part of the lesson.

The second AI-resistant part is the **output**, specifically the way the student is asked to defend it. This is where the assignment-design pattern reaches deliberately into older forms — the *crit*, the *oral defense*, the impromptu in-person justification, the *under-the-hood quiz* of what was made, the *artist's statement* — that have always been the standard assessment regime in trade school and art school, and that have sat outside the dominant humanities and sciences regimes until now. AI's pressure on the essay brings these forms back as load-bearing. In COMPLIT 126x, every project submission is a *system* plus an *artifact* plus a 500–700-word artist's statement, graded numerically against a rubric, that explains the design and reflects on what the project revealed about the discipline. In GENED 1196 (a large general-education course in folklore and performance), the output is an oral exam itself — a four-to-six-minute synthesis talk delivered to camera, with AI-generated follow-up questions, in a studio that captures a transcript the course can grade-norm against. In Becca Nesson's discrete math course, the output is a printed in-class follow-up that students complete in person the day after the take-home problem set is due; she scans the handwriting and uses generative AI to help sort and grade — a *low-tech solution with an AI back end*. In COMPLIT 166x, the capstone is the forged sixth memo of Calvino's *Six Memos for the Next Millennium* (the never-written one, on *Consistency*); the student submits the system, the memo, and a defense of both, with an oral component in which the instructor confirms the student, not the system, knows what was done and why. In every case, the output is not the AI's; it is the student's defense of the AI's role in their work.

Between input and output sits the system. This is where the substantive learning happens, and where a phrase the Lab has been refining — *prompt chains as a new form of close attention* — applies. A single prompt that says *write a passage in Calvino's voice* produces slop. The student breaks the act of producing the passage into named stages and operations: analyzing the corpus, identifying the formal moves, generating a draft, judging it against criteria the student has named, looping the judge over multiple candidates, surfacing the strongest. Each stage is a *move*. The student articulates what it is, what it requires the system to know, and what counts as a good versus a bad version of it. *Each negation of an iteration*, as Marlon Kuzmick (Director of the Learning Lab) has put it in the Lab's working notes, *makes the student increasingly conscious of some feature of the original.* The compare-and-contrast paper has always been the move humanities instructors reached for when students lacked an internalized corpus to read a single text against. The LLM's tendency to offer the *average of the internet* gives students that average corpus to compare against, and the iterative comparison against the canonical text becomes the place the close reading actually happens.

The pattern recurs at multiple scales across the courses the Lab is currently supporting. At the largest scale, COMPLIT 126x runs five projects across a fourteen-week semester, each a complete corpus → system → output → reflection cycle on a different disciplinary task. The other courses run versions of the same shape at compressed scales. COMPLIT 166x runs the prompt-chain component as a single capstone forgery. The *vibe-coding stations* — short, hands-on workshop sessions in which students use AI to write working code without needing to read it line by line — run a one-to-three-hour station-based version in a single class meeting. The *annotation activities* run the smallest classroom-scale version, in which students annotate a shared object (a map, a timeline, a syllabus) and the room queries the resulting database in the same meeting. At Harvard Law School, a civil-procedure course's TAs assemble a structured repository of casebooks, statutes, and prior course materials so that students' AI tools can draw from a vetted set rather than the open web — the *corpus engineering* prerequisite (the labor of cleaning, indexing, and converting source materials into machine-readable form) any pattern-instance requires. GENED 1196 runs the *defended output* at the scale of an eighty-student Gen Ed. Becca Nesson's discrete math course runs a one-page in-class follow-up that condenses the defended-output move into a single weekly artifact. Each is the same pattern, instantiated against the affordances of its course.

The pattern's pedagogical claim is that AI does not lower the rigor of undergraduate intellectual work — it demands more of it. A student designing a multi-stage research workflow, grounding it in a defensible corpus, naming and ordering its operations, evaluating each output critically, and synthesizing the whole into an artifact they can defend in person is being asked to do harder work than the traditional final paper required. The student is being asked, in particular, to make explicit what experts of the discipline have until now been able to leave implicit: what the moves of the discipline actually *are*. That articulation is most of the learning, and it is also what makes assessment possible: it lets a faculty member see, in a form that is not vibes-based, what a student thinks the thought processes of a discipline actually are.

What is still being worked out, on the design-pattern side, is the part that sits closest to faculty: rubrics for the artist's statement (sharper than current ones), protocols for oral defense in courses where it is not yet routine, scaffolds for the *input* phase that help faculty think through which embodied materials anchor their assignment, and the question of how much process documentation is right. The Bok Center's Summer 2026 faculty workshop sequence is being built around these questions explicitly, with faculty designing AI-native assignments through the same pattern, on materials they already possess (course readings, transcribed lectures, accumulated notes, years of correspondence with TFs about how to grade and respond to student projects).

What is *not* being figured out — because it has been figured out for a long time in the disciplines that have always used these forms — is the trade-school inheritance. The crit, the oral defense, the under-the-hood quiz, the artist's statement are not new pedagogical instruments. They are old ones whose role in the dominant humanities and sciences regimes diminished as the scaffolded essay rose. AI's pressure on the essay does not require inventing new assessment forms; it requires recovering forms the disciplines have always had at hand, and engineering them at the scale and granularity courses now need. The Bok Center's working synthesis is that the *re-engineering* of those forms — for scale, for fairness, for grade-norming, for student practice infrastructure — is what makes AI-resilient assignment design tractable in 2025–26 and beyond.
