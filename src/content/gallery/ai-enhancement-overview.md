Every Harvard student is now using AI in the chat. Our work this year was to build out a layer above that — assignments and activities that teach students the more advanced AI skills they will actually need as citizens, leaders, and knowledge workers, but to teach those skills in courses that are not "about" AI. The approach mirrors the way writing is taught at Harvard: students take a first-year writing course, then learn through writing across every discipline they study. We are doing the same for AI literacy. In comp lit, philosophy, anthropology, language, science, and media courses, students built context folders, designed prompt chains, constructed small systems of agents, and iteratively evaluated AI output against disciplinary exemplars. The skills that emerge — context engineering, structured prompt design, output evaluation, and agent management — turn out to be both genuinely transferable AI skills and powerful occasions for practicing the core intellectual work of each course.

The high-level justification for our AI Enhancement work this year is straightforward, even if its execution is not. Every student between the ages of five and twenty-five is already using AI in the chat. Our job, as a center for teaching and learning, is not to ratify or police that fact. It is to build the layer above it: the assignments, activities, and course structures through which students learn the more advanced AI skills they will actually need, and learn them in alignment with the learning objectives of the courses they are already taking.

This is the way other literacies have always worked at Harvard. Students take a first-year writing course, but they then learn *through* writing — and demonstrate their learning *through* writing — in essentially every other course they take. The essays students write are aligned with the learning objectives of those courses, because there is something about wrestling with ideas in prose that is the right way to develop and demonstrate ideas in anthropology, in literature, in history. The same is true for communicating with data in the sciences, where students learn to produce plots that surface the salient features of a result and to communicate their significance in the genre of a scientific paper. We are now doing the same thing for AI literacies, across all the disciplines and divisions, by designing assignments that align with the learning objectives of a course while also teaching the AI skills that increasingly matter.

The skills are not generic prompt-engineering tips. They are a structured set of practices that have emerged from current LLM use at the frontier and that map, with surprising directness, onto the kinds of things scholars have always done. As Becca has framed it in our conversations, the pattern looks like this: students learn to *form a context*; to *structure and index* that context; to design the *process* by which an LLM, or a small chain or system of agents, will operate on it; to *evaluate the output*; and to cycle back through all of those choices iteratively. This is, almost exactly, the shape of any serious piece of intellectual work — gathering sources, organizing them, designing a method, producing a result, and revising. Treating it explicitly as an AI workflow makes the underlying disciplinary reasoning unusually visible.

A comp lit example makes this concrete. In a course on Calvino, students were tasked with producing a "sixth unwritten memo" for the next millennium — the memo Calvino died before writing. To do this well, they had to construct a system of texts and prompts that would yield prose in alignment with Calvino's. That required intelligent choices about which texts to include, which to exclude, how much of each to include, and how to cross-reference them so that the LLM would attend to the features that mattered. All of that thinking required students to demonstrate mastery of a broad range of texts, to understand the relationships among them, and to make explicit decisions about foreground and background — learning objectives that have always been central to comp lit, and that the traditional essay has handled well, but that a context folder of this kind arguably forces students to engage at greater scope. The essay characteristically pushes students toward the local and the overlooked; building a working context folder out of the term's readings pushes them toward synthesis across the entire term.

A second pattern — prompt-chain assignments where the chain itself is the submitted artifact — extends the same logic. In courses where students built systems of prompts that performed an act of interpretation, translation, or analysis (analyzing oral histories in an anthropology course, generating a literary interpretation, executing a translation workflow with explicit context, steps, and evaluation), the *thought process* itself became the deliverable. Faculty no longer have to infer a student's reasoning from the polish of a final paper; the reasoning is laid out in the structure of the chain. There is almost no better way to show that a student has internalized the thought processes of a discipline than to require them to externalize those processes as a working system.

The third skill is iterative evaluation. As students develop a prompt chain, they do not just look at the output once; they continually compare it against an exemplar or test case and revise. This is a new kind of close reading — running, in effect, a thousand compare-and-contrast operations a day, attending to small differences between what the system produced and what the discipline would recognize as good work. Repetition of that kind, when aligned with course content, is a powerful teaching tool. It is also a rehearsal for the larger 21st-century shift the whole project is responding to: as the volume of AI-generated text and media rises, the ratio of human time spent producing versus evaluating is going to shift. If we want humans at the center of the operation, students will need to develop judgment, discernment, evaluation, and care as core capacities. In limit cases — reading MRI scans, screening for cancer — the human's job is to be the one in the system who is ethically and morally responsible for the output. That is a 21st-century skill, and it is one our courses can begin to teach now.

The Learning Lab's specific contribution was to take this framework — which can sound abstract — and to incubate it as an actual, repeatable design pattern. That work happened along an arc that runs from innovation, through incubation, to scale. At the innovation end, we worked closely with faculty on bespoke, course-specific assignments: a Calvino context folder, a translation pipeline in TS 280, vibe-coded playable components for a video-game essay in English 189, AI-assisted shot analysis in a Gen Ed on East Asian cinema, digital soundboards, data visualizations. In the incubation phase, our LUFs (undergraduate Learning Lab Undergraduate Fellows) tested those assignments rapidly, both to refine them and to generate sample student work across an array of disciplines, so that what began as a boutique design for one course could become legible to faculty in many. At the scaling end, we have been moving incubated patterns out — through TF training, through department-by-department workshops (the "road shows"), through faculty lunches, through Claude Code workshops with staff, and through the office hours that students drop into when they are actually building something.

The office hours and the LUFs together have done something that, in retrospect, may be the most important structural innovation of the year. Writing has, at the College, an entire stack of support: a first-year writing course, the writing center, peer writing tutors. The new modes of expression that AI has opened up — vibe-coded web apps, AI-augmented video essays, multimodal projects — do not yet have that stack. The Learning Lab is, in effect, building the missing stack. The office hours function as a kind of writing center for these new modalities: not technical support (the libraries handle that), not content advising (the teaching team handles that), but production-quality work on whether the storytelling and expressive choices students are making are good ones in this medium. The LUFs make this peer-supported in the way the writing center is peer-supported, and they make the assignments themselves better by surfacing misconceptions and disciplinary misalignments before the assignments reach the wider course.

There is also a research dimension we have begun to take seriously. A great deal of current ed-research framing asks unanswerable, overly broad questions — *is generative AI good or bad for learning?* The questions worth asking are far more local: *in this assignment, with these AI uses, which moves are supportive of learning and which substitute for the work students should be doing themselves?* Watching LUFs work through assignments closely, and revising the assignments in response, gives us actual information about that. It also gives faculty something they badly need: examples of student work and of well-aligned assignment designs they can adapt.

A final note about the assignment-design lesson the year has reinforced. The temptation, when AI is in play, is to leave assignments open-ended — to assume that the novelty of the medium will carry the activity. It does not. We had a course this spring where a vague, open-ended AI assignment left students unsure what they were doing and unable to hear that what they were attempting was misaligned with the course's learning objectives. The lesson is the same one any good assignment design rests on, only more so: an AI-enhanced assignment is a real assignment, with real learning objectives, real criteria, and real alignment work. The Learning Lab's job, with faculty, is to do that alignment work intentionally — to translate the instructor's learning goals into an activity structure that makes the AI use deliberate, iterative, assessable, and worth doing.

What the year demonstrates, taken together, is that AI is most valuable in teaching when it is taught through the discipline. Prompt design becomes a form of inquiry. Building a context folder becomes a way of practicing synthesis. A prompt chain becomes an externalized thought process the discipline can evaluate. Iterative output evaluation becomes a new kind of close reading. And the AI skills students need most — context engineering, structured process design, evaluation, agent management — turn out to be skills that the disciplines were already in the business of teaching, if we design the assignments to surface them.
