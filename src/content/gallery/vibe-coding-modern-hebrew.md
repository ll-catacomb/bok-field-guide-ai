For Modern Hebrew BB, the Learning Lab partnered with instructor Romy Neumark on a workshop in which students design and build their own AI-powered language-practice tools — 'tutorial machines' grounded in the research on how people learn second languages. The Lab assembled a curated reading list — the kind of brief on second-language acquisition, spaced repetition, deliberate practice, and the cognitive science of vocabulary learning that a teaching-and-learning center would normally hand to a faculty member — and gave it to students directly. With that context, the course's learning objectives, and the help of LLMs as comparative voice and memory scaffold, students prototype their own bespoke practice apps for whatever specific challenges they want to address: word-structure visualization, vocabulary automation, conversation prompts. In the spring of 2026, the Learning Lab partnered with Romy Neumark on a workshop for her Modern Hebrew BB students that turns one of the central anxieties about AI in higher education on its head. The workshop's premise is that students are going to use AI to learn languages — they already are — and the question is no longer whether to permit it, but how to teach them to do it well.

LLMs produce dramatically better outputs when grounded in high-quality, vetted material. So if students are going to use AI as part of their language learning, the workshop reasons, two kinds of grounding matter most. The first is accurate domain content: linguistically careful material about Hebrew itself. The second is meta-content — research on how people actually learn languages, the kind of material a teaching-and-learning center typically synthesizes for faculty rather than handing to students. The workshop hands students both.

Working with Anat Goldstein, an undergraduate on the Lab's team and a native Hebrew speaker who helped prototype the workshop's framework, the Lab assembled a curated reading list on second-language acquisition: spaced repetition, comprehensible input, deliberate practice, the cognitive science of vocabulary learning, and the conditions under which flashcard-style review does or doesn't actually help. Students get this reading list, the course's learning objectives, and a working environment for vibe coding — letting AI write substantial portions of code under their supervision and direction. From there, students design and build their own practice tools: word-structure visualizers, vocabulary practice automators, conversation-prompt apps, whatever specific linguistic challenge they most want to address.

The result is something radical: students get direct access to evidence-based knowledge about how they themselves learn — knowledge that, in the conventional pipeline, would travel from a research conference, through teaching-and-learning literature, through a teaching center's repackaging for faculty, through a faculty member's assignment design, and only then arrive at students in attenuated form. AI-assisted coding lets students short-circuit that pipeline. They engage with the research directly, react to it through the act of building, and get tools tuned to their own desires and feedback as learners.

The workshop is also a working answer to one of the more honest worries about AI in instruction: that AI will replace what humans do best in classrooms. It demonstrates the opposite. Hebrew instruction continues to do what it does — interaction, correction, modeling, the irreplaceable presence of a fluent teacher — and AI is reframed as a medium students learn to use well, in dialogue with both their instructor and the research about their own cognition. The Learning Lab plans to bring versions of this pattern to other language courses, and to extend the broader logic — students using AI to learn, faculty playing a guiding role in how — across many other parts of the curriculum.
