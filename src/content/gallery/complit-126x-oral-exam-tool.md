COMPLIT 126x / Hum 5 ends with an oral final in which students draw three questions from physical buckets, take fifteen closed-book minutes to prepare, and then defend their thinking face-to-face with an examiner — including a Big Question that always asks them to connect course readings to contemporary issues surrounding AI. To make that high-stakes format learnable, the Learning Lab partnered with Moira Weigel to build a practice web app that mirrors the exam's structure exactly: students draw two specific questions and one Big Question with its AI addendum, choose whether to take a fifteen-minute prep window, and then enter a fifteen-minute Q&A timer in which they record spoken responses to each question. After each response, Claude generates five candidate follow-up questions; a second "judge" call picks the strongest of those for the student to attempt. At the end, Claude reads through all the student's recorded answers and returns a coach's note — what was sharp, what's still finding its shape, what to work on — with no grades and no scores. The design treats AI as a study partner, not as an examiner, preserving the integrity of the in-person exam while making the kind of personalized rehearsal that traditionally required one-on-one office hours available at the scale of an entire course.

The Learning Lab and Bok Center's work on oral exams in 2025–26 has run on two parallel tracks. In one — GENED 1196 — AI sits inside the exam itself, transcribing student talks and generating follow-ups in real time so the format can scale to eighty students. The COMPLIT 126x / Hum 5 case is the inverse, and the inversion is the point. Here, the exam is deliberately, defiantly traditional. Students arrive fifteen minutes early, draw three slips of paper from physical buckets, take a closed-book preparation window, and then defend their thinking face-to-face with an examiner. There are no cameras, no transcription, no AI in the room. The technology that scales the exam lives entirely in the *practice* phase, before the student ever walks in.

That choice — keeping the exam human and using AI to scale rehearsal — was a considered one for Moira Weigel's course. COMPLIT 126x / Hum 5 builds across five units toward Big Questions like *Is the need to work a curse or a blessing?* and *What is a writer's voice?* — each appended, on exam day, with an addendum requiring students to connect course readings to "contemporary issues surrounding artificial intelligence." The exam asks students to speak about AI intelligently and fluently, drawing on texts ranging from Genesis and Hesiod to Mary Shelley, Freud, Walter Benjamin, and Claudia Rankine. To do that well takes practice — the kind of practice that, traditionally, has required individual office hours, a willing study partner, or a graduate student down the hall who has time to play examiner. None of those scale, especially in a course with a packed reading list and dozens of students.

The Learning Lab's response was to build Moira a custom web application that mirrors the exam protocol exactly. A student opens the app and draws the same three slips: two Specific Questions from the unit-tagged bucket (the system enforces Moira's rule that they cover different texts), and one Big Question with its AI addendum. They choose whether to take the fifteen-minute prep window. Then a fifteen-minute Q&A timer starts. For each question, the student records a spoken answer. When they stop recording, Claude generates five candidate follow-up questions tied to what the student actually said; a second "judge" call evaluates those five and surfaces only the strongest one, so the student is prompted by curated AI rather than raw AI. The clock pauses while Claude is thinking and while the student is replaying their own audio. At the end of the session — or when the timer runs out — Claude reads back through all the student's recorded answers and writes a coach's note: what was sharp, what's still finding its shape, a few concrete things to work on. No grades, no numbers.

The pedagogical case for AI here is precise. AI is not testing the student; it is *rehearsing* with the student. That distinction is what lets the practice tool be ambitious about its assistance — multiple takes, immediate follow-ups, an end-of-session synthesis — without compromising the integrity of the live exam. AI plays the role a graduate study partner would play if the course had a graduate study partner per student to spare, which it does not. And the candidate-and-judge architecture is itself a teachable artifact: it embodies exactly the kind of prompt-chaining and context-engineering practices the course's workshop earlier in the term taught students to design and critique. The students who use this tool are using a working example of the design principles they have been studying — high-signal context, deliberate decomposition, narrowly scoped output — applied to the problem of helping them prepare for a humanities exam.

The work also addresses a less-discussed scaling problem in higher education: the gap between what oral assessment *can* test and how much practice students typically get to develop the skills it tests. Speaking under time pressure, with notes but not a script, is a learned competence. Most syllabi treat that competence as something students should already possess; few build in serial rehearsal opportunities. The practice tool makes serial rehearsal cheap and personalized, and it does so without putting more grading load on the faculty member or her teaching staff. Moira's exam still feels like Moira's exam — three slips, fifteen minutes, a real person across the table — and the apparatus that makes it learnable is something the student touches in private, on their own time.

This is the Bok Center's AI-augmented oral exams initiative articulated in a different register from the in-the-exam case. AI is the connective tissue, not the substance. Where GENED 1196 demonstrates that AI can make the *delivery* of an oral exam scalable, COMPLIT 126x demonstrates that AI can make *preparation for* an oral exam personal at scale. Read together, the two projects begin to sketch the design space for a Bok-supported faculty member who wants to assign serious oral assessment without the format's traditional limits on enrollment, integrity, or fairness.
