Oral exams have long given faculty a window into students' authentic, AI-proof understanding, but their labor-intensiveness and the inability to revisit performances after the fact have kept them rare in large courses. In GENED 1196, a General Education course on folklore and performance, the Learning Lab built and tested an oral exam workflow in which AI played a structural role — transcribing each session in near real time, generating three personalized follow-up questions tied to each student's specific arguments, and capturing every artifact (drawn cards, transcripts, follow-ups) in a queryable database. Students drew thematic cards from three concept decks, prepared briefly, and delivered spontaneous synthesis talks; follow-ups were delivered half in a human voice and half in a synthetic voice as a research variable. Eighty students completed the pilot, with survey data (90%+ response rates) showing strong pedagogical gains alongside meaningful friction with the recording and AI elements. The pilot models a direction for the Bok Center's broader Teaching and AI work: AI not as a threat to assessment integrity, but as the connective tissue that lets faculty assign rigorous, performance-based assessments at scale.

For decades, oral exams have been an open secret in higher education: rigorous, AI-proof, generative of authentic insight into student understanding — and almost impossible to scale. The bottlenecks are familiar. A faculty member running one-on-one vivas for eighty students is functionally a graduate seminar with one teacher, and grading rests on memory and notes that no second reader can review. Without an artifact, there is no way to norm scores across evaluators or respond to a grade dispute with evidence. The format, in other words, has historically been an artisanal pleasure rather than a standard assessment.

The Bok Center's Learning Lab partnered with the GENED 1196 teaching team to test whether AI integrated into the assessment workflow — not used to write or grade student work, but used to make oral exams *runnable* at General Education scale — could change that calculus. The course was a deliberate choice. Its curriculum on folklore and performance already includes audio ethnography, oral storytelling, and analysis of performance, so unscripted oral synthesis assesses the same literacies the syllabus teaches. The pilot also extended the Learning Lab's longer history of multimodal experimentation, in which video, audio, and embodied modes of student work have been treated as primary forms of evidence rather than supplements to writing.

The design was anchored by a Learning Lab studio equipped with forward and overhead cameras (capturing both the talk and the student's own scratch notes), a proctor for troubleshooting, and a prompt system built around three thematic card decks: Core Concepts, Case Studies, and Theoretical Models. Each student drew four cards from each deck, took five minutes to prepare a presentation linking up to five of them, and delivered a four-to-six-minute spontaneous talk. The novel element was what happened next. The system transcribed the talk in near real time and generated three personalized follow-up questions tied to the specific arguments the student had just made. Half the follow-ups were delivered by a human voice and half by a synthetic voice, held constant across all eighty exams, allowing the team to study how question modality affected student response. None of the follow-ups were graded; their purpose was to deepen comprehension and create a reflective artifact for the student and for the course.

The pedagogical case for AI in the stack rests on three affordances the team designed for explicitly. First, *norming.* Because every session produced a transcript, multiple graders could review the same artifact, calibrate against shared rubrics, and respond to grade disputes with evidence — capabilities a traditional oral exam, governed by human memory alone, simply cannot offer. Second, *scale.* Automated scheduling, capture, and follow-up generation reduced the human time-cost per exam by enough to make eighty exams feasible within an academic term, where the format had previously been the province of small seminars or graduate students. Third, *reflective iteration.* The AI-generated follow-ups, anchored in each student's specific talk, functioned as personalized post-exam learning prompts and gave instructors a corpus of student-specific responses to review for future course design.

Two reflective surveys (n=74 and n=75, both above 90% response rate) showed strong pedagogical signal alongside meaningful friction. Eighty-six percent of students agreed that exam preparation deepened their understanding; eighty-one percent felt the topics accurately reflected course content; seventy-three percent judged the difficulty appropriate. Satisfaction was more split: sixty-nine percent reported overall satisfaction, but only fifty-five percent said they would choose the format again for a future final. The AI-component survey found that seventy-five percent of students felt comfortable interacting with AI during the Q&A, and sixty-eight percent said AI-generated questions helped them connect ideas — but fifty-one percent found the questions confusing or hard to follow, a result the team attributes largely to the audio-only delivery condition (no on-screen text), which a future iteration will address.

Qualitative feedback clarified that student resistance centered on the apparatus needed for scaling — being recorded, the use of AI, the studio setting — rather than on the oral assessment itself. One student wrote: "I liked the structure and content...However, I didn't like that it was recorded. I would much prefer to just sit down and talk to a person." That tension is the pilot's central design lesson: the technology that makes the format scalable is also its most visible source of student discomfort, which puts the burden on future iterations to make the studio feel less like a recording booth and more like a conversation.

More broadly, the work models a stance the Learning Lab and Bok Center are taking toward AI across the curriculum: not as something to remediate against in writing assignments, but as infrastructure that makes high-integrity assessment formats — oral exams chief among them — practical in courses where they had been impossible. AI-as-scale-enabler, rather than AI-as-threat, is a frame that turns the conversation about assessment integrity from defensive to generative, and the GENED 1196 pilot offers a concrete model for what that frame produces in practice.
