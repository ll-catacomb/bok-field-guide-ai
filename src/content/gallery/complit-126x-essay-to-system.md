The Bok Center Learning Lab's *essay-to-system* design pattern — articulated abstractly elsewhere as **corpus → system → output → evaluation** — runs in extended form across COMPLIT 126x / Hum 5, *Literature and/as Artificial Intelligence*, a course whose title is also its argument: that literature itself is a kind of AI, with its repository of human experience and its variety of forms for recombining them, and that imitation has been the test of machine intelligence since Turing. Across five units — Work and Automation, Voice and Style, Alignment, Artificial Persons, and Originality and Multimodality — students do not write a final paper; they ship five working AI systems, one per unit, each tied to a discipline-specific Skill the unit names explicitly (distant reading and structured output; close reading and prompt chaining; historicist reading and custom knowledge bases; psychoanalytic reading and multi-agent system design; intermedial reading and diffusion-based multimodal translation). Each project follows the same four-step engineering: students *assemble a corpus* of primary texts, photographs, or recorded speech; *decompose the disciplinary act* (writing in a poet's voice, simulating a Frankensteinian character, generating an ekphrastic chain) into named moves; *build the system* in a Python notebook the Learning Lab has scaffolded for them; and *defend the result* in a 500-to-700-word artist's statement graded numerically against a rubric while the artifact itself is graded complete/incomplete. The Learning Lab supports the course with three pieces of teaching infrastructure: (1) a from-scratch *Python textbook for literature students* whose seven notebooks introduce coding through humanistic operations (repetition and formula, motifs as buckets, compression and loss, schemas and validation, agency and action); (2) a *workshop notebook* sequence of paired *start* / *complete* files used in section meetings to teach a single skill at a time (functions, loops, API calls, structured output, cards, multi-agent platforms, diffusion); and (3) per-project section materials, including a Lovesongs folder with a notebook per assignable poet (Shakespeare, Yeats, Brooks, Cavafy, Merrill, Sexton, Rich, O'Hara) so students can begin Project 2 with a working scaffold for whichever poet they have chosen. The rest of the Bok Center's AI-integration portfolio — the Calvino seminar, the HLS materials work, the AFRAMER oral-history station, the HUM 350 graduate pedagogy seminar — runs versions of the same pattern at compressed scales; 126x runs it across a full term.

The Bok Center Learning Lab's design-pattern work has a recurring move at its core: take what the scaffolded final paper used to do — teach a discipline, assess whether the student learned it, and produce a finished artifact recognizable to the discipline — and rebuild that work as a system the student designs, runs, and defends. Across 2025–26 this pattern has been deployed in many forms — a one-hour vibe-coding station, a single capstone forgery of Calvino's missing sixth memo, a corpus-engineering repository at HLS, a one-class oral-history workshop in AFRAMER 223, a faculty-development session for HUM 350. COMPLIT 126x / Hum 5 is the version where the pattern runs for a full fourteen-week semester, across five sequential projects, with a custom teaching infrastructure built for it.

The course's title is its argument: *Literature and/as Artificial Intelligence*. The double preposition is the move. Literature is treated both as the object generative AI is now operating *on* — generating sonnets, simulating personas, producing ekphrastic chains — and as itself a kind of AI, with its repository of recombinable human experience and its history of imitation as a test of intelligence. The reading list runs from Genesis and Hesiod through Shakespeare, Mary Shelley, E. T. A. Hoffmann, Freud, Kafka, and Ben Lerner, and ends with Claudia Rankine's *Citizen* — and at every stage the AI projects exist to deepen and extend the literary attention the course is asking students to bring to those texts, not to substitute for it.

The pattern is identical across all five projects, even as the technical apparatus and the disciplinary Skill change unit to unit:

**Step 01 — Assemble the corpus.** This is up to the student. For Project 1 it is the *King James Bible* and a sweep of ancient and medieval automata texts (Homer, Hesiod, Aristotle, Plato, Valmiki, Lie Yukou, Vitruvius, Hero of Alexandria, al-Jazari). For Project 2 it is at least three additional poems by a poet the student has chosen as an influence, plus their selected exemplar from class. For Project 3 it is what a Frankenstein-character could plausibly have read or known — a defensible claim, not a guess. For Project 4 it is the prompts, backstories, conversational constraints, and contextual framing that govern how multiple custom chatbots will speak to one another. For Project 5 it is the student's own photograph of a museum object plus a recorded ekphrasis. *A traditional lit review asks the student to find sources they will cite. This asks them to find sources who will be structural to their system.*

**Step 02 — Decompose the disciplinary act.** A single prompt that says "write a love song in Anne Sexton's voice" produces slop. The student breaks the act of producing the artifact into stages and operations: feature extraction on the Sexton corpus, identification of recurring images and syntactic habits, production of a draft lyric, judgment against criteria the student has named, optional looping. Each stage is a *move*. The student articulates what it is, what it requires the system to know, and what counts as a good versus a bad version of it.

**Step 03 — Build the system in a Lab-scaffolded notebook.** The Learning Lab has built teaching infrastructure for this step in three pieces. A *Python textbook for humanities students with no prior coding experience* runs across seven notebooks: `00_python_basics` (zero to one API call), `01_types_and_containers`, `02_repetition_and_formula` (distant reading via word-frequency, n-grams, hapax legomena, comparing two texts), `03_motifs_as_buckets` (clustering words into themes), `04_compression_and_loss`, `05_schemas_and_validation` (structured output), and `06_agency_and_action` (agents). A separate sequence of *workshop notebooks* — paired *start* / *complete* files — teaches one skill at a time: functions, loops, API calls, structured output, cards, multi-agent platforms, and diffusion. A *Lovesongs* folder ships a notebook per assignable poet (Shakespeare, Yeats, Brooks, Cavafy, Merrill, Sexton, Rich, O'Hara), so a student who has chosen Anne Sexton begins Project 2 not from a blank cell but from a working scaffold pre-populated with Sexton-specific corpus operations. A *multimodal-systems* folder ships analogous scaffolds for Project 5's image-text-image diffusion chain. A *section-notebooks* folder ships per-class-meeting applications, including the Project 1 Encyclopedia notebook used during the Learning Lab section in week three. None of this teaching apparatus is content-locked to comparative literature.

**Step 04 — Run, read, fix.** The first version of any project will be flat in places, fluent over uncertainty in others, possibly wrong about the source materials in ways that are interesting. The student reads it the way the discipline would read it: noticing where the cadence breaks, where the conceptual move was borrowed from a different writer entirely, where the model has confused multiplicity with mere accumulation. Then they change the corpus or the prompt chain or the system architecture, and run it again. Across the five projects, the iteration loop is where much of the disciplinary learning happens — *seeing the model fail at the discipline's specific moves* (the model that gets Sexton's tonal register wrong, or that confuses what Frankenstein's creature could plausibly have read with what Mary Shelley happened to cite) is what makes those moves explicit.

**Step 05 — Defend.** Every project submission is a *system* plus an *artifact* plus a *500-to-700-word artist's statement* that explains the design and reflects on what the project revealed about the discipline. The creative artifact (the encyclopedia entries; the album of lyrics and the Suno track; the chatbot transcript; the multi-agent dialogue; the multimodal sequence) is graded complete/incomplete. The artist's statement is graded numerically against a rubric. The structural effect is that the student is graded on the explicit articulation of what the system is, why it was designed that way, and what the disciplinary lesson is — the part of the work AI cannot produce, because the artist's statement has to defend the specific corpus and specific decomposition the student in fact made.

Across the five units, the Skills the syllabus names map directly onto the AI techniques each project asks the student to operationalize:

- **Project 1: Encyclopedia of Ancient Robots** — distant reading, structured output. Students extract features from a sweep of ancient automata texts and historicize the practice within Bacon's, Blair's, and Busa's lineage of reading-as-information-management. The deliverable is at least ten encyclopedia entries with images, plus a statement on the traits used to determine what counted as a "robot" and the advantages and disadvantages of structured output as a reading method.

- **Project 2: Artificial Love Songs** — close reading (including scansion), prompt chaining, music generation. Students pool poems by a chosen poet, extract style features, build a chain that produces new lyrics, and generate a song in [Suno](https://suno.com) in an appropriate musical style. The deliverable is an album's worth of lyrics, a sound file, and a 500-700-word essay on the prompt chain and what the LLM got right and wrong about poetry.

- **Project 3: Interview with a Monster** — historicist reading, custom knowledge-base construction. Students assemble a corpus a Frankenstein-character could plausibly have read or known and build a chatbot that speaks in that character's voice, then run a structured interview that explores how address, first-person language, and stylistic coherence generate the illusion of life. The deliverable is the interview transcript and a 500-700-word reflection on how voice, persona, and historical context interact.

- **Project 4: The Agent Ensemble (Theater of the Future)** — psychoanalytic reading, multi-agent system design. Working in groups of two or three, students design *the conditions under which dialogue is generated* — prompts, backstories, conversational constraints, contextual framing — and observe how characters maintain or drift away from intended personas, how recursive bot-to-bot conversation amplifies tendencies toward agreement, escalation, theatricality, mysticism, or incoherence, and how breakdown functions as a dramatic event. The deliverable is the bot-to-bot transcript and a 500-700-word essay on dramatic authorship, persona drift, and the ethics of unstable artificial characters.

- **Project 5: Imaginary Museum (Ekphrasis, Iteration, Multimodal Translation)** — intermedial reading, diffusion. After a visit to the Harvard Art Museums, students photograph an artwork and record themselves speaking about it, then run an iterative chain — ekphrasis → image → description → negation → image — multiple times. The deliverable is a curated sequence of outputs and a 500-700-word reflection on ekphrasis, reproduction, originality, and authorship; selected projects are displayed in a public location on campus through reading and exam period.

The rest of the Bok Center's AI-integrated portfolio runs versions of the same pattern in compressed form. The COMPLIT 166x Calvino seminar runs the prompt-chain component of 126x's Project 2 as a single capstone forgery. The MOD-HEB BB workshop runs a one-hour version of the Project 2 *vetted-content* logic, in which the LLM produces the wrapper around content the student has already vetted rather than generating the language content itself. The AFRAMER 223 oral-history workshop runs a single-session version of the 126x *corpus assembly + presentation surface* move. The HLS *civil-procedure-materials* repository runs the corpus-engineering prerequisite that any 126x-style integration depends on. The HUM 350 graduate humanities pedagogy seminar runs a one-class version of the assignment-design step itself, with future faculty designing AI-integrated assignments in the same shape.

A piece of infrastructure that does not always get named in writeups about courses like this, but is structurally essential: the Lab's full-stack support apparatus around the course. Students arriving with no prior coding experience encounter real friction — installation that doesn't work the way the documentation said it would, an API call that errors silently, a model that produces something the student cannot evaluate without help — and the workshop alone is not sufficient infrastructure for that friction. The Lab's office hours are. The Faculty Director's analogy: the Lab's office hours are *a writing center for AI-augmented and multimodal academic work* — peer and near-peer support staffed by Bok Graduate Fellows (BGFs) and Learning Lab Undergraduate Fellows (LUFs) who are not there to do the technical work for the student, and not there to do the disciplinary content work either, but to help the student answer the question *is this a good version of whatever the thing is in this medium that I have been asked to produce.* That production-quality role, sitting between the teaching team's domain expertise and the libraries' technical expertise, is what lets a *rigorous multimodal assignment* — as opposed to an *open-ended creative assignment* — function as a real assignment. The disciplinary stakes of each 126x project make production-quality matter at every step.

A related piece of infrastructure sits *behind* the course: the LUF rapid-iteration and ed-research track. New for the Lab as a center, this two-pronged activity has the LUFs (a) running through draft activities and assignments before they are deployed in the course, surfacing misconceptions and giving the Lab feedback on how long a stage actually takes; and (b) producing sample student work across an array of disciplines so that when the Lab meets with a faculty member who is considering adopting the architecture in their own field, there is a tangible, discipline-adjacent artifact to show. The LUF curriculum is, increasingly, where the Lab's outward-facing resources for the *here is what an AI-enhanced assignment can look like* framework are taking shape. Many of the design choices visible in 126x's project sequence were stress-tested against LUF cohorts before they reached the course's roster.

The structural claim the course supports: any discipline whose intellectual work can be decomposed into named moves, and whose primary materials can be assembled into a defensible corpus, can run this pattern. 126x has now run it across five disciplinary registers within a single course — distant reading on the Bible and ancient automata; lyric voice on twentieth-century love poetry; historicist reconstruction on Frankenstein; psychoanalytic dramatic theory on multi-agent dialogue; intermedial translation on diffusion-model image chains. The question for an adjacent humanities course becomes not whether the pattern *could* run there, but what the specific named moves of that discipline are, and what corpus its students would assemble to operate on them. The Learning Lab's role going forward is to bring the same notebook curriculum, the same scaffolded sequence of skills, and the same five-step engineering to whichever course or department asks the question next.
