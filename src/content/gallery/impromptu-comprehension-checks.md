Take-home problem sets, response papers, translations, and short essays remain pedagogically valuable even when generative AI is widely available — but only if instructors can be confident that the submitted work reflects the student's own understanding. The Bok Center is supporting a range of faculty in adopting a shared strategy: keep the take-home assignment, but add a short, impromptu check shortly after it is submitted, in which students must explain, extend, or reflect on what they handed in. The check can be written on paper at the start of class (as in COMPSCI 20), conducted as a brief structured oral exchange (as in FRENCH 10/11, HIST-LIT 93AA, and GENED 1196), or delivered as a live Q&A immediately following a student-produced video (as in SCRB 111), and is increasingly supported by Bok Center–prototyped software that uses AI to generate follow-up questions tied to each student's specific submission. The most effective check questions are themselves pedagogically productive — asking for metacognitive reflection, extension, or transfer rather than rote factual recall — so that the check advances student learning while creating a strong incentive for students to deeply understand the work they submit. The result is a format that is lightweight, scales well, and reliably surfaces gaps between submitted work and demonstrated understanding, while changing how students approach the take-home work itself. The work is anchored in the Bok Center's *Oral Assessment and Homework Verification* working group, which is producing concrete guidance for faculty across the FAS.

When generative AI made it trivially easy to produce credible-looking take-home work, faculty across the FAS faced a familiar dilemma. The take-home formats they had relied on for decades — problem sets, translations, response papers, short essays — remained pedagogically valuable, but it had become harder to know whether the work submitted reflected the student's own understanding. The two obvious responses each came with significant costs: keep the assignments unchanged and grade work whose authorship was uncertain, or replace them with in-class formats that crowded out the kind of extended, reflective work that take-home assignments are uniquely good at producing.

The Bok Center is helping faculty adopt a third path: keep the take-home assignment, and pair it with a brief, impromptu, in-person check administered shortly after submission. The format is deliberately low-stakes and low-friction. It is not a re-test of the assignment; it is a verification that the student can stand behind what they handed in. The checks come in several flavors, calibrated to course size and discipline.

It is worth being explicit about what this strategy is and is not. It is not a mechanism for preventing students from using generative AI as part of their out-of-class process. Realistically, we can't prevent AI use. It is a mechanism for motivating them to reach a particular endpoint: one in which they deeply understand the work they submit and are ready and able to explain, defend, extend, and apply it. Whatever path a student takes through the take-home assignment — including one that involves AI — the check is what defines the endpoint that has to be reached.

In **COMPSCI 20: Discrete Mathematics** (Rebecca Nesson), students submit traditional problem sets and then complete a short, paper-based, in-class follow-up that asks them to explain a step in their own proof, extend a result to a small variant, or reflect metacognitively on which problem was hardest and why. The follow-ups take a few minutes to complete and a few minutes to grade, and are highly effective at surfacing cases where a student has submitted work they cannot reconstruct.

In **FRENCH 10/11** (Nicole Mills), the pattern is adapted to language instruction. Students complete take-home written work and are then asked, in class, to produce or discuss the language live — without a translator or chatbot at hand. Brief structured oral exchanges, sometimes supported by prototype comprehension-checker software, let the instructor verify that the linguistic competence on the page matches the linguistic competence in the room.

In **HIST-LIT 93AA** (Lauren Kaminsky) and **GENED 1196** (Sarah Craycraft), the check is more substantively oral. Students who have submitted reading responses or course writing meet in short, structured oral follow-ups in which they articulate the argument they have committed to in writing and answer questions about how they arrived at it. In GenEd 1196, oral assessment is the central assessment format, not an add-on — and it scales to a large enrollment because of a piece of software the Bok Center has prototyped that uses AI to generate fresh follow-up questions tied to each student's specific submission. Rather than asking every student the same canned set of questions (which would be easy to prepare for and circulate), the system reads the student's submitted work and produces individualized prompts that probe the actual claims and reasoning that student has made. This makes oral assessment both more rigorous and more practical at scale.

In **SCRB 111: Regenerative Biology** (Jessica Whited), the same AI-supported follow-up pattern is applied to a different submission format altogether: student-produced video. As a final project, student pairs produce roughly five-minute videos translating a topic in stem cell or regenerative biology for a general audience — a task Whited frames not as a dumbing-down of course content but as a rigorous test of how well students understand it. After each in-class screening, students answer questions generated live by a Bok Center–developed AI pipeline that extracts six to eight claim anchors from the video's transcript, drafts lay-audience-calibrated questions, and runs a "referee pass" to align tone and rubric before the questions reach the student. The questions are answered live, in front of peers. Whited reports that all six presenting groups in Fall 2025 used AI somewhere in their production process and all six found the assignment more rewarding than a traditional final exam — and that the format resists AI misuse on its own merits, with one student observing that it is "difficult to 100% chat a video you have to defend live."

The *content* of the check matters as much as the format. Across these pilots, the most effective prompts are themselves pedagogically productive: they do not test whether the student can recite a definition from page 1 of their submission, but rather ask the student to reflect on their own reasoning ("which step in your proof are you least sure about, and why?"), to extend a result to a small variant ("how does your argument change if we drop this assumption?"), or to transfer a technique to an unfamiliar case ("apply the approach you used in Problem 2 to this situation"). Questions of this kind do real intellectual work for the student. They cannot be answered well without genuine understanding of the submitted work, and they advance the student's learning in the very moment of the check. They also create the right incentive: the surest preparation for a metacognitive, extension, or transfer prompt is to actually do the thinking the assignment was designed to elicit, rather than to outsource the production of the artifact and hope for the best. The Bok Center's prototype comprehension-checker software is being trained, in effect, on this principle — the AI-generated follow-up questions are calibrated to elicit reasoning rather than recall, and the Oral Assessment and Homework Verification working group is building a shared library of question patterns that instructors across disciplines can adapt.

A few patterns hold across these pilots. First, the checks are diagnostic without being adversarial: a student who genuinely did the work can almost always answer; a student who did not, generally cannot. Second, when the check questions are designed for reflection, extension, or transfer rather than factual verification, the check is itself a piece of teaching — students leave the room having thought harder about their own work, not just having been audited on it. Third, the cultural effect on student behavior is substantial: the presence of well-designed checks changes how students approach the take-home assignment in the first place, focusing them on the reasoning the assignment is meant to develop rather than on the production of a clean-looking artifact. Fourth, the format is faculty-adaptable: each instructor designs the check that fits their pedagogy, and the Bok Center supports the design, the AI-generated question prototype where useful, and the cross-course learning that turns multiple separate pilots into shared FAS practice.

This work is anchored institutionally in the Bok Center's *Pressing Pedagogical Challenge* working group on **Oral Assessment and Homework Verification**, convened with Kaminsky, Craycraft, and other faculty. The group is producing concrete guidance — on question design, documentation, fairness, student anxiety, and grading consistency — that other instructors across the FAS can adopt. The aim, in the working group's framing, is not to police AI use but to strengthen authentic assessment.
