The scaffolded essay carried three professional functions on a single artifact, for most of the past century: it taught a discipline, it assessed whether the student had learned it, and it matched the form of academic communication the professor was already producing in their own research. The condition that held that alignment together is no longer entirely intact, and the assessment function in particular has come loose. The Learning Lab's response, articulated through the Bok Center's broader Generative AI initiative, has been to design workshops in which students *decompose* the tacit intellectual moves of their discipline and *externalize* them — using AI to write the code that would otherwise stand between them and the artifact — into systems whose components correspond to those moves. The repeating shape is a hands-on station-based session of one to three hours in which students ship a small working artifact (a playable game mechanic, a scrollytelling essay, a digital gallery, a prompt chain, a Python notebook, a deterministic practice app, a montage micro-argument, an AI-integrated assignment design) tied to the course's own primary materials, present it, and defend the design choices behind it. Two industry techniques the workshops teach — *context engineering* (assembling and indexing the text the model needs to see) and *multi-agent system design* (breaking an intellectual task into named sub-tasks at a level of precision an agent can act on) — map onto disciplinary moves the curriculum was already trying to teach: the lit review and the explicit articulation of how an argument, translation, or interpretation works. The deliverable across the twelve courses is a class of student work that is harder to fake than an essay and that defends a specific decomposition of the discipline's moves the student in fact made.

The required first-year writing course at Harvard is roughly 150 years old, and the intellectual technology at its center — the scaffolded academic essay — carried three professional functions on a single artifact for most of that span: it taught students how to think within a discipline, it assessed whether the student had learned, and it matched the form of academic communication the professor was already producing in their own research. Student learning, faculty assessment, and disciplinary publication all rode on the same object.

The condition that held that alignment together is no longer entirely intact. When the artifact a student turns in can be produced by a tool the student has access to and the instructor cannot reliably detect, the inferential link between the artifact and the student's learning weakens. Faculty across the curriculum are now in the position of needing to decompose, more explicitly than they have had to in a century and a half, what the essay tacitly carried — and to rebuild those functions across new combinations of instruments. One of the workshop architectures the Learning Lab has been deploying toward that rebuilding is what staff have come to call, internally, the *vibe-coding station*.

The shape is consistent across the dozen courses we have run it in. Students arrive with a course-specific creative or analytical task whose tacit moves are familiar to expert practitioners but rarely articulated explicitly: writing in the voice of a specific author, demonstrating how a film's three-point lighting works, presenting an oral history, translating a Korean poem, designing a language-practice activity, situating a protest song within a wider historical frame. Over the course of the workshop, in groups of two to four, students decompose the task into named operations, assemble a curated context (a corpus of primary materials, the course's own theoretical apparatus, the student's own analytical notes), and use an LLM to *vibe-code* — Karpathy's term for letting AI handle the syntax while the human directs in natural language — a small working artifact whose components correspond to the moves they have just articulated. The artifact is the deliverable. The articulation is the learning. The defense is the assessment.

Two AI techniques the workshops teach map directly onto disciplinary moves the curriculum was already trying to teach. *Context engineering* — assembling and indexing every passage that needs to be present in the AI's working memory for a task — is a much more demanding version of what the lit review was supposed to do. *Multi-agent system design* — decomposing a complex intellectual task into distinct sub-tasks at a level of precision an agent can act on — requires students to articulate explicitly what it means to construct an argument, translate a text, or write in the voice of an author they are studying. This articulation is most of the learning. It matters for teaching, because it gives students access to the structure of what they are learning. It matters more for assessment, because it lets a faculty member see, in a form that is not vibes-based, what a student thinks the thought processes of a discipline actually are.

The twelve deployments are not all doing the same depth of work, and the Lab's own thinking distinguishes two tiers. In the first tier — internally, the *toes-in-the-water* deployment — the LLM essentially writes a small, self-contained piece of software the student describes in natural language: a playable jump-mechanic widget for a video-games essay (ENG 189), a scrollytelling cinema sequence with frame-specific highlights (GENED 1049), a deterministic flashcard or grammar-drill app populated with vetted course content (MOD-HEB BB). The multimedia-expression case for these is straightforward: students get to *video-game about video games*, or *animate about cinematography*, or *practice Hebrew with a tool they themselves designed* — and the essay-only version of the same disciplinary argument was going to be clunkier than the demonstration. The student describes a thing; the model writes the code; the student adjusts colors and copy. The threshold of going from "I have never opened a terminal" to "I shipped a working app" is meaningful for many students. Context engineering, decomposition, agent design, and iterative output evaluation are not what these deployments exercise at depth.

In the second tier — the *process* tier — the workshop pulls students into the full corpus → decomposition → system → output → evaluation cycle, with the disciplinary task as the substrate. TS 280 sits at the hinge between the two tiers. Graduate students in translation began with a low-tech move: writing the steps of their own translation practice on physical cards (literal gloss, register negotiation, idiomatic compression, historical context retrieval, etc.), one move per card. The Lab photographed those cards and used vibe coding to convert each card into a single cell in a Python notebook, so the student could see their own translator's mental model running as a computational pipeline. *The discipline's own tacit process* — externalized first onto cards, then into code — became the system. COMPLIT 126x and COMPLIT 166x sit further along: students go through the same corpus → system → output → reflection cycle repeatedly across an entire term, on increasingly demanding disciplinary tasks. The toes-in-the-water tier is, in practice, an on-ramp to the process tier; students who ship a small playable widget sometimes come back asking *can I make a larger version of this in office hours,* once they have recognized that the small artifact is part of a larger system worth building.

What follows is a non-exhaustive list of the courses in which this workshop architecture has run during the 2025–26 academic year. Each instance is shaped by its own discipline's primary materials and tacit moves, and the entries are roughly ordered along the toes-in-the-water → hinge → process spectrum.

- **ENG 189 — *Video Games as Cultural Texts*.** Students vibe-coded *playable* components for their multimodal essays. An essay arguing about jump mechanics, for example, embeds a playable iframe in which the reader can adjust gravity, jump-power, and velocity to *see* the argument operate. The artifact is the demonstration; the essay's claims become falsifiable in a way text-only argument cannot make them.

- **GENED 1049 — *East Asian Cinema*.** Students vibe-coded scrollytelling essays with frame-specific highlights, and three.js scenes that demonstrated three-point lighting, shot setups, and other compositional choices. The course's central interpretive moves — close looking, attention to mise-en-scène — became spatial and inspectable rather than only verbal.

- **AFRAMER 223 — *Storied Lives: Methods in Oral History*.** Students vibe-coded presentation surfaces for their ethnography and oral-history work: digital galleries, interactive sites with embedded clips and animations, data-visualizations of recurring themes across interviews. The disciplinary move being externalized was the curatorial-and-analytical one of placing testimony in interpretive context.

- **TS 280 — *Translation Proseminar*.** Graduate students externalized their tacit translation processes onto physical cards (one card per move: literal gloss, register negotiation, idiomatic compression, etc.); the Learning Lab then photographed those cards and used vibe coding to convert each card into a single cell in a Python notebook. The notebook is a machine-translation pipeline shaped to the student's *own* translator's mental model — a way of teaching machine translation by forcing alignment with the discipline's own schema rather than around it.

- **COMPLIT 126x / Hum 5 — *Literature and/as Artificial Intelligence*.** Multiple workshops over the term: vibe-coded encyclopedias of "ancient robots" pulled from Genesis and ancient Greek texts; "love song" generation chains that emulated specific poets through prompt chaining; museum field-trips in which students performed an LLM-mediated operation on physical inputs to produce new outputs. The workshop architecture became the course's own central pedagogy.

- **COMPLIT 166x — *Italo Calvino Seminar*.** Students vibe-coded the path from a curated primary corpus, through a designed prompt chain or agentic system, to a generated output (a passage of new text in Calvino's voice, written for the course's capstone forgery of Calvino's missing sixth memo on *Consistency*). Calvino's own description of literature as a "combinatorial machine" became, in this workshop, a literal one.

- **MOD-HEB BB — *Modern Hebrew Beginning*.** In a single hour, students vibe-coded *deterministic* practice apps and widgets — flashcard interfaces, vocabulary drills, grammar quizzes — populated with vetted course content. The metacognitive move being externalized was the pedagogical-design one: what kind of practice actually helps me learn? The model's role was not to generate language content (which it gets wrong) but to generate the wrapper around content the student had already vetted.

- **HISTLIT 93AH — *Music, Politics, and Protest in the United States*.** Students used AI as a discussion catalyst at hands-on stations that annotated printed lyrics and ran background agents over newsreel footage and historical write-ups, clustering data points to surface scholarly gaps and candidate paper topics. The workshop produced a more capacious mental model of the field as the deliverable; the artifacts — annotated lyrics, clustered patterns — were both individually inspectable and collectively analytical.

- **HUM 350 — *Humanities Pedagogy Seminar*.** A graduate-level workshop in which the *students themselves* (future faculty) practiced designing AI-integrated assignments that preserved rigor, originality, and humanistic inquiry — i.e., they vibe-coded the kind of assignment they will later assign. The recursion is the point: the workshop architecture taught itself, with the seminar's own students as the next generation of adopters.

- **SLAVIC 192 — *The Cinema of Stanley Kubrick*.** Students worked directly with film clips to use montage as a tool of analysis, isolating, cutting, and juxtaposing moments to identify recurring visual patterns. AI support was framed as an optional aid rather than a substitute for interpretation; the artifact was a small "micro-argument" built through montage, accompanied by a working inventory of clips and a low-friction media-manipulation workflow students could carry forward.

- **TDM 196SS — *The Singer-Songwriter*.** A station-based workshop on podcast composition, video essays, and soundbed design. Students rotated through hands-on activities that practiced integrating voice with music, building image-based arguments, and composing or manipulating sound to support claims about songs tied to their final-project ideas. The discipline's tacit move — making argument and aesthetic act on each other — was the thing being externalized into media.

- **MUSIC 160R.** Multimodal essays, immersive experiences, sound-boards and synth-board interfaces — digital tools that students built either to *describe* music in argumentative form or to *make* music through novel digital affordances. The workshop ran the same architecture as the others, with the discipline's primary materials being audio and the tacit moves being performance-and-listening practices that the students rendered into interactive form.

What recurs across the twelve is a class of student work that makes a discipline's tacit moves explicit, makes the student's mastery of those moves visible, and produces an artifact close enough to a real disciplinary form to be defendable. The workshop is one piece of connective tissue between the course's primary materials and that artifact. Where the Learning Lab once added a single workshop to a course as a high-engagement supplement, it is now contributing the architecture by which courses can rebuild what the essay is no longer fully able to carry.
