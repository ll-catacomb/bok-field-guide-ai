The traditional rhythm of an annotation activity is annotate → hand in → wait → get a graded copy back days or weeks later. The Learning Lab's annotation pattern collapses that rhythm into a single class meeting: students annotate a shared object whose entities (places on a map, dates on a timeline, readings or weeks on a syllabus) are already mirrored in a database; their marginalia, circles, and short comments are extracted via a vision model into structured records keyed to those entities; and the class queries the resulting dataset before the meeting ends. The room can ask which reading the most students interacted with this week, which place on the map drew the heaviest annotation, where attention converged and where it dispersed — and answer the question with evidence the room produced together that morning. The pattern has been deployed across NELC courses (against maps and timelines whose entities are mirrored in a working scholarly database) and in **ANE 140** and **HISTLIT 90FK** (against syllabi, for capstone-project preparation), and it preserves a property the deeper Bok-supported integrations also rely on: the artifact is at once a learning vehicle, an assessment instrument, and structured data the course infrastructure can act on. Unlike the vibe-coding stations, this pattern requires no student coding and drops into a single existing class meeting; the infrastructural lift is on the course side, and once the scaffolding is in place individual class meetings can use it without further setup. The Lab's internal name for what makes this work is *paper as user interface*: AI is the connective tissue between physical annotation and queryable data, not a model the student is prompting directly.

The traditional rhythm of an annotation activity is annotate, hand in, wait, get a graded copy back days or weeks later. The teaching value of the activity is real — close reading, marginal commentary, evidence-based marking — but the *reflective* value is deferred, often until after the next class meeting has already moved on. The Learning Lab's annotation pattern collapses that rhythm into a single class meeting, by ingesting the annotations into a database that mirrors the content being annotated and giving the class the affordance to query that database before the meeting ends.

The pattern began as a prototype in COMPLIT 207 in academic year 2024–25 and has since been redeployed across academic year 2025–26 in NELC courses (multiple deployments against maps and timelines), in ANE 140 (syllabus annotations for capstone-project preparation), and in HISTLIT 90FK (the same syllabus-annotation pipeline). It was also one of the lightning-round demonstrations the Lab walked Google through during their May 2026 visit, where Madeleine described the underlying move as *paper as user interface* — AI tools are often imagined as exclusively digital, and the annotation activity is one place the Lab is pushing back on that assumption.

In its general form, a student is asked to annotate a shared object — a map, a timeline, a syllabus, a reading — by hand on a printed sheet or directly in a digital interface tied to a database. The annotations are then ingested: directly from the digital interface, or from photographs of the physical sheets passed through a vision model that extracts marks, marginalia, underlines, circles, and short written comments into structured records. Those records land in a database that mirrors the content being annotated — its places, its dates, its readings, its sections — so that a student annotation about a particular city on a map, or a particular week on a syllabus, becomes addressable as data linked to that entity. Once the annotations are addressable, they are queryable. The class can then ask: *what reading did the most students interact with this week? which place on the map drew the heaviest annotation? where did the room collectively converge, and where did it diverge?* The query step happens in the same class meeting as the annotation step. The room is no longer waiting on a TF to read and aggregate sixty annotated copies of a reading before the next session; the aggregation is mechanical and fast, and the picture of the room's reading is something the room can discuss together while the meeting is still in progress.

The pattern has run in two distinct forms across the year. In the NELC deployments, students annotate maps and timelines whose underlying entities — geographic features, sites, periods, named events — are already represented in a scholarly digital database. The annotation activity does two things at once: it teaches the substantive content of the course (this place, this date, this connection), and it produces records keyed to entities the field already studies as data. Because the database mirrors the content of the visual artifact, an annotation made on a printed map lands in the database against the right site without the student having to do any data-entry work, and students wind up working with the structured representation scholars use without taking a separate course in data structures to get there — the structure is provided, and they annotate *into* it. The database is enriched across cohorts: each year's annotations accrue against the same entities, so the room's relationship to the material is layered on top of every prior room's, and the historical record of where past rooms have converged becomes itself something a class can read.

In ANE 140 and HISTLIT 90FK, the deployment was on syllabus annotations for capstone-project preparation. Students annotated a printed syllabus — circling readings they wanted to engage with, marking passages, writing short notes in the margins — and the annotated syllabi were photographed and sent through a vision model. The model extracted the annotations into structured records linked to the specific reading or week they appeared next to, and those records flowed into a database the class queried at the end of the same meeting. The questions the room can ask of this database are unusually direct: which reading the most students interacted with, what patterns appear across one student's annotations over the term, where in the syllabus the room is converging and where it is spread out. These are not new questions to a careful instructor; what is new is that the answer is grounded in evidence the whole class produced together that morning, and the act of producing it was itself the lesson.

The pedagogical work the pattern does is several things at once. It closes the loop between activity and reflection within a single class meeting, so the room sees its own collective reading reflected back to it before the meeting ends rather than after it has moved on. It teaches students that their marks on a page are data they can interrogate, and that move — once made — is available to them in every subsequent reading practice they take up. It gives the instructor a finer-grained read on the room than other activities support: a quiz tells you what students know, a discussion tells you who is willing to talk, but annotation patterns queried in the moment tell you where the *whole* room's attention actually went, including the patterns no one would have articulated even if asked. And it demonstrates a property the rest of the Bok-supported AI portfolio also relies on, at a smaller scale: the artifact is at once a learning vehicle, an assessment instrument, and structured data the course infrastructure can act on. The same property that makes the multi-agent assignments in COMPLIT 126x coherent — the artifact is *itself* the articulation of the thinking — operates here in a single class meeting.

The pattern is also a low-floor entry point for instructors not running full-stack student systems. Annotation activities of this kind require no student coding, no semester-long sequence, and no rebuild of the syllabus. They drop into an existing course meeting. The infrastructural lift is on the course side — the database schema, the vision-model ingestion path — and once that scaffolding is in place, individual class meetings can use it without further setup. The same instructor who runs the activity once is in a position to recognize it as a candidate for other course artifacts whose content mirrors a database: readings indexed by passage, image corpora indexed by tag, problem sets indexed by step, primary sources indexed by entity.

What this pattern shares with the rest of the Bok-supported AI work is a single architectural move: lift the latent material of the course into a form the course's tools can act on. The maps and timelines were already part of the substantive content of the NELC courses; the syllabi were already part of capstone preparation in ANE 140 and HISTLIT 90FK. What changed is that those materials — and the students' engagement with them — became *addressable*. Once addressable, they could be queried; once queryable, they could be discussed in class as evidence; once discussable, they became a new kind of pedagogical object the courses now reach for. The same move runs underneath the COMPLIT 126x corpora, the HLS *civil-procedure-materials* repository, and the GENED 1196 oral-exam transcript pipeline — at much larger scales of artifact, but with the same underlying property. The annotation activity is the smallest classroom-scale instance of it, with the round-trip from student mark to queryable record to in-class discussion completed inside a single meeting.

A few directions worth flagging for future deployments. The cross-cohort accumulation in the NELC courses now amounts to a multi-year database of annotated maps and timelines, and the question of how to put that accumulated record in conversation with each new cohort — show them where past rooms have converged, ask them where they diverge — has not yet been worked out. The capstone-syllabus deployment gives the instructor a view of each student's annotation pattern; whether and how to give the *student* that same view, as a piece of metacognitive feedback on their own reading practice, is worth deciding before further deployments. The pipeline depends on the vision model reading marginalia accurately, and the failure modes (illegible handwriting, ambiguous symbols, marks falling between two entities on the page) should be characterized so future deployments can decide where to keep the activity on paper and where to move it into a digital interface. And the pattern is not specific to maps, timelines, or syllabi: any course artifact whose content is mirrored in a structured database is a candidate, and naming the property explicitly — *the artifact mirrors a database* — makes it easier for instructors to recognize when a candidate activity is in front of them.
