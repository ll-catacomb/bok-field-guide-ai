The Bok Center is one of Harvard's faculty support units, and like any operation it has to think seriously about how to use AI to become more efficient and more effective at what it does. The Learning Lab in particular has deployed generative AI in a number of novel ways this year: not as a replacement for the high-touch consulting and studio work we have always done, but as a way to remove friction from the manual technical processes that used to bottleneck multimodal course work. Many of those friction points have been completely transformed — especially anything that touches transcripts, translations, or the conversion of raw faculty materials into the structured formats Canvas and other delivery platforms expect. Two strands run through this work. First, our internal practice has gotten dramatically better: we can support a Modern Hebrew course in real time without staff who speak the language; we can document everything said and drawn in a studio session and turn it into student-facing async resources; we can spin up a custom course website per project where we used to make a PowerPoint. Second, every one of these workflows is a pilot — designed from the start to be extracted from the studio and put in the hands of faculty, who do not need to be physically in the Lab to benefit from the same techniques in their own scholarship and teaching.

In addition to our consulting work with faculty on courses where they and their students will be using generative AI, the Bok Center — like any of Harvard's support units — has to run like any other operation, and that means thinking seriously about how to use AI to become more efficient and more effective at the work we do. The Learning Lab has been the most active site of that thinking this year. We have deployed generative AI internally in a number of novel ways, both to make our current work more efficient and to create entirely new capabilities that benefit Harvard's faculty and students by enabling things that simply weren't doable before.

The clearest place to see the transformation is in our long-standing support for multimodal communication. We have always worked with faculty whose students need to communicate about visual or quantitative data in forms beyond text — through images, animations, video, audio — or to analyze films, music, and other non-textual sources. The technical labor of producing this kind of work used to be heavily manual and frequently the bottleneck. That technical work has been completely transformed by generative AI, especially anything that touches transcripts and the operations that can be performed on them.

A concrete example: in language courses where students do a final presentation in the target language, our staff in the past had to lean on the instructors and course-affiliated speakers to do the editing work, because we did not necessarily know the language. Now we generate transcripts that are accurate down to the millisecond, with translations alongside them, and that makes it possible for our editors to extract exactly the moment a student wanted captured — without ever having understood the original audio at the level of meaning. This past term, in a Modern Hebrew course, we went further: we used live translation to support students' studio activities in real time, without forcing them to break out of the target language to communicate with our staff in English about their media projects. That single change is transformative for what a language course can ask of the studio.

A second strand has been around capturing and reusing teaching content. We have built workflows where faculty and staff can record video drafts of their teaching — or pull in previous terms' lecture videos — and then use AI to transcribe, clean, and database the content so that the underlying intellectual material can be harvested for course modules, handouts, and student-facing materials. In partnership with Hewitt, we have been exploring the Canvas API to automate the move from those source materials into a properly built-out Canvas site. Anyone who has tried to assemble a Canvas course from a pile of Google Docs, scattered slide decks, and handwritten notes knows the friction this removes. The faculty material that previously required hours of manual reformatting flows into Canvas with most of the structural work done for them.

A third strand is one a great many people across knowledge work are now noticing: things that used to require commissioning or buying an app can now be done with AI-assisted coding, often more cheaply, more directly, and more precisely targeted to a particular client or project. Where in the past we would have made a PowerPoint deck, a Keynote, or a Word handout, it is now relatively easy to spin up a custom website for every course that comes through the studio — with the materials presented in more effective interactive forms, and with the whole thing remixable and translatable as we work with course after course after course. That capacity raises the floor for both async student resources and live in-class slide support.

It also pays off for accessibility, in the broadest sense. We are now capable of documenting every single thing that happens in our studio — every word spoken, every diagram drawn — and then describing all of it using AI vision models and language models. That means students with different access needs, students who simply weren't able to make it to a session, and students who want to look back at a class later all get a richer record than would ever have been feasible to produce by hand. There is also a slightly paradoxical pedagogical effect that has surprised us: when we can credibly tell a class, *don't worry about taking notes — we'll be taking notes the whole time and sending them to you*, students can spend more time engaging in the lived experience of being in class. They aren't furiously copying URLs from a slide or duplicating what's already on screen in their notebook. They can pay attention. They can think.

All of this so far is about us — what the studio can now do that it couldn't do a year ago. The more important point, and the reason this story belongs in a year-end report and not just an internal memo, is that every one of these workflows is a pilot. We are designing them from the start to be extractable from the Learning Lab studio. In our work with faculty who are exploring AI to enhance their own teaching, we have begun to build out workflows and tools that don't require physical presence in the Lab to use. A faculty member can get transcriptions of any lecture they have ever recorded. They can learn how to prompt well and how to ground a model in the right context for their discipline. Once they know how to plug these capabilities into the data sources they actually have — Google Drive, lecture archives, lab notebooks, syllabi — and into the delivery platforms that matter to them, especially Canvas, the result is not just efficiency. It is higher-quality instructional content, because faculty are now able to focus their preparation time on what really matters intellectually rather than on the thankless infrastructural work that used to consume it.

This is, in miniature, the same argument the rest of our AI Enhancement reporting is making. AI is not interesting because it makes things faster. It is interesting because, used well, it lets the academic content of a course — the disciplinary substance, the actual ideas — sit at the center of student and faculty attention. The Learning Lab studio is where we have been trying that argument out on ourselves first.
